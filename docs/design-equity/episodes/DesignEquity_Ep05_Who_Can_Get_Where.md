## Transcript

Transportation is access. It connects people to work, education, food, care, family, recreation, and public life. Transportation is also exposure. Routes and projects distribute traffic danger, pollution, noise, cost, travel time, and displacement pressure.

This episode adapts Kristine Miller's Transportation Equity chapter under CC BY 4.0. The narration condenses and paraphrases Miller's work. It excludes third-party reports, statistics, images, quotations, and linked media.

Evaluate a trip from door to destination, not one vehicle or facility in isolation. A bus route is not accessible if the path to the stop is unsafe. A short distance is not convenient if a traveler must make multiple transfers. A bicycle facility is not useful to everyone if gaps occur at the most dangerous locations.

Use six tests: affordability, safety, reliability, accessibility, time, and exposure. Apply them to different travelers and trip purposes. Include people who do not drive, people with disabilities, older adults, youth, shift workers, caregivers, and households with limited income. Do not treat any group as uniform.

Transportation benefits and burdens can fall in different places. A project that saves drivers time may increase crossing distance or exposure for nearby residents. State the tradeoff instead of hiding it in an average.

Build an access table. Select essential destinations. Estimate total time, money, transfers, walking or rolling distance, schedule constraints, and failure risk for each travel mode. Add qualitative evidence about comfort, harassment, shade, seating, wayfinding, and perceived safety.

Engagement must reach riders where and when they travel. A meeting held after service ends excludes the people most affected. Report back with route or design changes and explain unresolved constraints.

Torrance application segment. This original segment is separate from Miller's chapter. Its cited local source is the official Torrance Transit website, checked October seventh, twenty twenty-six. Routes, fares, and schedules can change, so current service information must be verified.

Choose a hypothetical trip between a Torrance home area and an essential destination. Compare driving, transit, walking or rolling, and bicycling where feasible. Include first and last segments, transfer risk, operating hours, and accessible paths. Repeat the comparison for a worker with a late shift and a traveler using a mobility device.

Identify one infrastructure change, one service change, and one information change. Name which agency controls each action. This is a study method, not a finding about Torrance Transit performance or legal compliance.

Transportation equity asks who can get where, under what conditions, at what cost, and with which burdens. The answer should be measured at the scale of a person's complete trip.
