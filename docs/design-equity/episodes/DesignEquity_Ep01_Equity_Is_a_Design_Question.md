## Transcript

Every designed place distributes something. A sidewalk distributes safety and mobility. A park distributes shade, recreation, and public investment. A zoning map distributes permission and constraint. A meeting notice distributes information and the practical chance to participate. Design equity begins by asking who receives the benefits, who carries the burdens, and who has power over the choice.

This episode adapts chapter one of Kristine Miller's Introduction to Design Equity, published by University of Minnesota Libraries Publishing and available through Engineering LibreTexts under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0. The narration condenses and reorganizes Miller's ideas and adds a separate Torrance application. Third-party readings, images, quotations, statistics, and linked media from the chapter are excluded.

Equality and equity are not interchangeable tests. Giving every neighborhood the same object can look equal while leaving unequal access untouched. An equity analysis starts with conditions and asks what each community needs to reach a fair result. It also asks how present conditions were produced. A missing sidewalk, distant grocery store, dangerous crossing, or inaccessible meeting is not simply an isolated defect. It can be part of a pattern of decisions.

Design is larger than professional styling. Policies, services, forms, schedules, budgets, buildings, and streets all shape behavior. Their effects can differ from their stated intent. A decision-maker therefore needs an impact question as well as an intention question. Who can use this? At what cost? At what time? With what risks? Who cannot use it, and why?

Use a four-column equity ledger. In the first column, name the decision. In the second, list expected benefits and burdens. In the third, identify groups and places likely to experience each effect. In the fourth, record who helped define the problem and who can change the proposal. Do not treat demographic categories as explanations. Pair quantitative patterns with direct community knowledge and document uncertainty.

Equity is also procedural. Inviting comments after the major choices are fixed is different from sharing power while alternatives are still open. Track which ideas changed the project, which did not, and why. That decision trail makes participation testable.

For a hypothetical element workshop, apply the equity ledger before selecting a preferred policy. Compare who gains access to housing, jobs, public facilities, cleaner air, and safer travel under each alternative. Identify burdens such as displacement pressure, noise, traffic exposure, or reduced access. Then publish the assumptions and invite correction. This exercise does not judge Torrance's legal compliance or adopted policy.

The practical lesson is simple. Equity is not a decorative value added after design. It is a method for defining the problem, distributing power, comparing effects, and checking outcomes.
