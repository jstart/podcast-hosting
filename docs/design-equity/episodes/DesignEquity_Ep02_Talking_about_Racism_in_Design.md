## Transcript

Design equity requires language precise enough to describe unequal conditions without turning a difficult conversation into a contest over personal character. Racism can operate through rules, institutions, accumulated investment, and routine professional practice, including when no participant states a discriminatory intent.

This episode adapts Kristine Miller's chapter Learning to Talk about Racism from Introduction to Design Equity, licensed CC BY 4.0. It paraphrases and reorganizes Miller's text. Third-party readings, videos, images, quotations, and statistics are excluded.

Begin by separating four things: intent, action, pattern, and outcome. Intent asks what a person hoped to do. Action asks what the decision actually changed. Pattern asks whether similar decisions repeatedly advantage or burden particular groups. Outcome asks who receives access, safety, wealth, exposure, or exclusion. Intent matters, but it cannot answer the other three questions.

Use specific nouns and verbs. Say which policy, boundary, investment, design standard, or engagement rule is under examination. Say what evidence shows. Avoid using a whole community as a symbol, and avoid expecting one participant to speak for everyone who shares an identity.

Discomfort is not the same as harm. A productive process can acknowledge discomfort while keeping attention on the public decision. Ground rules should protect participation without preventing evidence-based discussion of race and racism.

An equity discussion needs more than aggregate averages. Ask how results vary by race together with income, disability, age, tenure, language, and location when reliable data allow. Small numbers require privacy protection and caution. A disparity is a prompt for investigation, not automatic proof of one cause.

Create an evidence ladder. Start with the observed condition. Add historical and policy context. Add community accounts. Identify plausible mechanisms. Test alternatives. Then state what remains unknown. This keeps the conversation analytical and prevents certainty from outrunning evidence.

Torrance application segment. This is original local analysis, separate from Miller's chapter. The cited source is the official City of Torrance General Plan page, checked October seventh, twenty twenty-six.

Imagine reviewing outreach for the Land Use and Safety Element update. Do not ask only whether everyone received the same notice. Ask whether language, schedule, location, disability access, digital access, and trust affected who could respond. Compare participation with the population affected, but do not reduce representativeness to a quota. Publish the outreach methods, summarize themes without erasing disagreement, and show what changed.

If a racial pattern appears, describe it accurately and examine possible policy mechanisms. Do not assign motives without evidence. Do not claim the exercise determines legal compliance. Current law, facts, and qualified advice control any real decision.

The goal is not perfect vocabulary. It is a disciplined public practice: name the decision, distinguish intent from impact, examine patterns, protect participants, and remain open to correction.
