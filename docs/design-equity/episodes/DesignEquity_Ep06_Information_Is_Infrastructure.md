## Transcript

People cannot participate in a decision they cannot discover, understand, or use. Information equity concerns access to information and also whose knowledge counts when a problem is defined and a solution is chosen.

This episode adapts Kristine Miller's Information Equity chapter under CC BY 4.0. It condenses and paraphrases Miller's text while excluding all third-party readings, images, quotations, statistics, and linked media.

Information has a delivery chain. An agency creates it, selects a format, publishes it through channels, and expects a person to find, understand, trust, and act on it. Failure at any link can exclude participation. Posting a long technical document online is not equivalent to making its meaning accessible.

Test four dimensions. Availability asks whether the information exists in time. Accessibility asks whether language, disability access, device, bandwidth, and format barriers are addressed. Comprehensibility asks whether a reader can understand the decision and consequences. Agency asks whether the person can respond before the choice is fixed.

Information also flows toward government. Residents hold place-based knowledge about timing, routes, indoor conditions, informal networks, and past implementation. Treating that knowledge as anecdote while treating every dataset as complete creates a false hierarchy.

Create an information audit. List every audience, channel, format, language, publication date, response method, and responsible staff role. Test the notice with people who did not draft it. Provide a plain-language summary without replacing the full technical record. Preserve disagreement in summaries.

Close the loop with a response table. For each major comment, state whether the proposal changed and why. Protect personal information and explain data limits.

Torrance application segment. This is original local study, separate from Miller's chapter. The source is the City of Torrance General Plan page, checked October seventh, twenty twenty-six.

For a hypothetical Land Use and Safety Element workshop, design an information path from first notice through final decision. Include a concise project description, map alternatives, accessible files, language access, telephone and in-person options, and a clear deadline. Explain what is open for change.

After engagement, publish themes, corrections, staff responses, and the next decision point. Do not claim that this exercise describes the City's actual outreach or determines legal sufficiency.

Information equity turns communication into public infrastructure. The standard is not whether a file was posted. The standard is whether affected people had a fair chance to know, understand, contribute, and see the result.
