## Transcript

Design thinking is often presented as a cycle of understanding users, defining a problem, generating ideas, making prototypes, and testing them. Equity asks harder questions before that cycle begins: who framed the problem, whose experience is treated as expertise, and who controls the final choice?

This episode adapts Kristine Miller's chapter What Is Design Thinking and What Does It Have to Do with Equity, licensed CC BY 4.0. It paraphrases Miller's work and excludes the chapter's third-party article, graphics, quotations, and linked media.

A technical solution can miss a political or economic cause. If healthy food is unaffordable, redesigning a store interface does not solve income inequality. If a dangerous street reflects repeated budget priorities, a temporary demonstration may reveal an option but cannot substitute for durable authority and funding.

Add four gates to the design cycle. Reflection asks what assumptions and limits the team brings. Context asks how history, policy, and power produced the condition. Shared definition asks affected people to shape the problem and success measures. Accountability asks who owns the decision, resources, data, and follow-through.

Prototypes can reduce risk, but experimentation has burdens. Do not test on a community without consent, safeguards, a way to stop, and a commitment to report results. Compensate expertise where appropriate and lawful.

Write a problem statement with three layers. Describe the observed condition without assigning cause. Identify systems and decisions that may contribute. Then state whose desired outcome defines success. Revisit the statement after engagement.

Compare alternatives with distributional measures, not only total benefit. Record who gains, who pays, who may be displaced, and who maintains the result.

Torrance application segment. This original exercise is separate from Miller's chapter and cites the City of Torrance General Plan page, checked October seventh, twenty twenty-six.

Imagine a design sprint about access to a public facility. Begin with a reflection memo and a local decision map. Invite affected users to revise the problem statement before generating options. Test accessible, low-cost prototypes only after explaining what can and cannot change.

Publish the evaluation criteria before choosing. Include access, safety, maintenance, displacement risk, and community assessment. This is independent study, not a report on Torrance practice or a legal conclusion.

Design thinking becomes more equitable when it exposes assumptions, places the problem in history, shares definition and power, and remains accountable after the prototype.
