## Transcript

Land-use decisions shape where homes, industry, roads, parks, food, public facilities, and hazards are located. Environmental justice asks whether pollution and health risks are concentrated, whether health-supporting benefits are accessible, and whether affected communities have meaningful power in the decisions.

This independent study draws from the California Department of Justice Bureau of Environmental Justice's September twenty twenty-three Best Practices for Implementing Senate Bill one thousand, the DOJ's current SB one thousand page, and current California Government Code section sixty-five thousand three hundred two. It is not legal advice or a compliance determination.

The DOJ guide explains that discriminatory land-use practices have contributed to unequal pollution burdens. Current general-plan law requires qualifying jurisdictions to identify disadvantaged communities, engage them, and address environmental justice through specified policies and objectives. Exact duties depend on current law and facts, so the live statute and qualified counsel must guide any real application.

Use a three-part frame. Distribution asks where burdens and benefits fall. Procedure asks who receives information, participates, and influences the decision. Recognition asks whether the analysis respects community knowledge and distinct conditions instead of treating every place as interchangeable.

Begin with a land-use pathway. Identify a decision, such as a designation, buffer, facility investment, or transportation policy. Trace how it changes exposure, access, housing stability, physical activity, food access, or civic participation. Then identify which public body controls each part of the pathway.

Do not treat environmental justice as one map layer. Screening data can guide inquiry, but local evidence, history, and lived conditions are necessary. State assumptions and uncertainty.

Torrance application segment. This segment is separate from the DOJ source. It cites the official City of Torrance General Plan page, checked October seventh, twenty twenty-six. That page says the City is updating its Land Use and Safety Elements and will integrate environmental-justice goals and policies.

For study, select one hypothetical policy alternative and create a distribution, procedure, and recognition memo. Identify possible pollution burdens and health-supporting benefits. List affected communities without declaring that any area meets a legal definition. Describe how community evidence could change the alternative.

This exercise does not evaluate Torrance's plan, determine whether a statutory trigger applies, or substitute for current City records and city-attorney advice.

The core lesson is that land use is an environmental-justice issue because public decisions shape exposure, opportunity, and voice over long periods.
