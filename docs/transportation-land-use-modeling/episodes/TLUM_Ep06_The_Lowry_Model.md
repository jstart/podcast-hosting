## Transcript

The Lowry model is an influential framework for allocating urban activities. It divides employment into basic and service sectors. Basic employment is treated as an external driver serving a wider market. Households locate in relation to employment and available land. Service employment then locates in relation to households. The process repeats until changes become small.

Accessibility connects the pieces. Zones that are easier to reach from jobs may attract more households, subject to capacity and other constraints. Population creates demand for local services, which creates more employment and another allocation round. Distance or travel impedance reduces interaction.

The model is useful because its logic is clear, but its simplifications are substantial. The basic-service distinction can be difficult in a complex economy. Household types, prices, development feasibility, zoning, remote work, and multiple travel modes may be represented only roughly or not at all. Calibration can make a base year fit without guaranteeing a good forecast.

The source chapter uses the Lowry model as both a method and a foundation for later integrated models. Its enduring lesson is iterative interaction between employment, population, land, and transportation.

Torrance application. This separate conceptual example cites the Torrance General Plan. Imagine dividing Torrance and nearby areas into analysis zones. Regional or export-oriented employment would be identified as an initial input. Residential capacity would reflect adopted land-use assumptions. Travel impedance would connect workplaces and possible household locations. Population would then support an estimated amount of local service employment.

The exercise would need regional geography because workers and jobs cross city boundaries. It would also require several household and employment categories, multimodal access, land prices or feasibility, and explicit capacity constraints to avoid an unrealistic allocation.

Results should be tested against observed population and employment, then varied under alternative assumptions. A converged allocation is not a prediction. It is the consequence of the inputs and behavioral structure.

No Lowry model is estimated here, and no forecast is offered for Torrance. The application is a teaching example showing what local data and limitations would have to be addressed.
