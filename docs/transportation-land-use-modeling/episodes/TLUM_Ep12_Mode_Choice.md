## Transcript

Mode choice estimates how travelers divide among available ways of making a trip. The source chapter places this step after trip distribution in the traditional four-step model, while also recognizing that mode, destination, time, and other choices can interact. A mode is not truly available merely because it appears in a network file. The traveler must be able to reach it, afford it, use it safely, and complete the trip at the required time.

Analysts describe alternatives through generalized cost. This can combine in-vehicle time, waiting, walking, transfers, fares, tolls, parking, reliability, and other burdens. Different travelers value those attributes differently. Income, age, disability, household responsibilities, vehicle access, trip purpose, and time constraints can all affect choice.

Discrete-choice models represent each alternative with a utility function. A logit model converts relative utility into probabilities. The result is not a statement that every traveler makes an identical calculation. It is a structured estimate of how observed choices relate to measured attributes and unobserved variation.

The choice set is critical. If the model offers transit where service does not operate at the needed hour, the estimated probability is misleading. If walking access, transfers, parking search, or reliability are omitted, the model can favor the mode whose burdens are least completely represented. Market segmentation can improve interpretation, but it requires adequate data and must not turn demographic averages into stereotypes.

Calibration and validation should compare observed and modeled shares by purpose, time, geography, and relevant traveler group. Policy tests should change the actual service or cost variable, then report sensitivity instead of treating the result as guaranteed behavior.

Torrance application. This separate local segment cites the Torrance Transit Tomorrow Plan and the City's Multi-Use Active Transportation Trail project page. Consider a hypothetical scenario connecting neighborhoods, employment, Downtown Torrance, and the Regional Transit Center.

The choice set would use actual route frequency and span, transfer conditions, fares, roadway times, parking, and safe walking, rolling, and bicycling access. A trail connection could change first-and-last-mile time or comfort, while a service change could alter waiting and reliability. Those effects should enter as documented scenario assumptions, not as automatic mode-shift claims.

The analysis should distinguish residents, workers, visitors, people without vehicles, and travelers with disabilities where suitable data allow. It should retain a nondigital way to participate in surveys and should protect privacy. Results could be shown across several values of time or service assumptions to reveal uncertainty.

No ridership estimate, mode-share forecast, or benefit finding is made here. Current local data, calibrated parameters, project details, and agency review would be required. The exercise shows how mode choice connects service quality and complete-trip access to a transparent scenario.
