## Transcript

Trip distribution connects the trip productions and attractions estimated in the first model step. It produces an origin-destination pattern: how many trips travel between each pair of zones for each purpose and time period. The source chapter presents growth-factor methods and gravity models as two major approaches.

Growth-factor methods begin with an observed trip table and expand it to match future totals. They can be practical when the future resembles the base year, but they tend to preserve existing patterns and perform poorly for new zones or major structural change. A method that reproduces the past neatly can still miss a future shift in land use, network access, or travel cost.

The gravity model relates interaction to the amount of activity at origins and destinations and to the impedance between them. In plain language, larger opportunities tend to attract more trips, while greater time, cost, or difficulty tends to reduce interaction. Friction factors represent that deterrence. Different trip purposes can have different distance or time distributions.

Calibration adjusts friction factors and other parameters so modeled trip lengths and interchange patterns resemble observed data. The analyst should compare more than one regional average. A model can match mean trip length while missing important corridors or population groups. Validation should examine screenlines, district flows, trip-length distributions, and results by purpose.

The output is conditional on zone boundaries, travel-time skims, land-use totals, and the treatment of trips that begin or end outside the modeled area. Uncertainty in those inputs carries into every later step.

Torrance application. This separate local segment cites the City of Torrance Transportation Analysis Requirements for Private Development. For a hypothetical development study, trip distribution would connect the estimated project trips to destinations and gateways using current evidence and the method accepted for the study.

A reviewer should identify whether the distribution comes from observed turning movements, regional model patterns, nearby comparable uses, market information, or a combination. The record should state the base year, study period, external gateways, and any manual adjustment. It should also explain how construction, remote work, unusual count days, or a changed network affect transferability.

The model geography matters in Torrance because travel crosses municipal boundaries and because employment, commercial, industrial, residential, and freight activities generate different patterns. A coarse regional zone may need local refinement, but local detail should remain consistent with regional totals.

Sensitivity testing could compare plausible destination patterns rather than hiding uncertainty in a single percentage. The analyst should keep this circulation question distinct from the separate environmental vehicle-miles-traveled analysis described in City guidance.

No origin-destination result or project finding is offered here. Current City requirements, observed data, and project-specific direction would be necessary. The application demonstrates how to expose the evidence and assumptions behind a trip-distribution table.
