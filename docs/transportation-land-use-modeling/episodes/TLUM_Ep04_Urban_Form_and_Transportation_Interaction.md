## Transcript

Urban form describes the spatial arrangement of people, jobs, activities, buildings, and networks. The source chapter examines how concentrated, corridor, polycentric, and dispersed patterns interact with transportation. Density alone does not determine travel. The location and mix of destinations, street connectivity, transit quality, parking, and regional structure all matter.

Agglomeration can increase productivity and access by bringing related activities together. It can also increase land cost and congestion. A polycentric region may shorten some trips by distributing employment, yet generate complex cross-travel if housing and jobs do not match. Network design shapes which of those destinations are practical by walking, transit, or car.

Job accessibility is more informative than the number of jobs nearby. It combines travel impedance, competition, mode availability, and worker characteristics. A job may be geographically close but inaccessible because the shift begins before transit service, the route is unsafe, or the worker lacks the required mode.

Models should therefore represent several scales. Neighborhood form affects local trips and station access. Regional form affects employment and long-distance travel. Equity analysis asks whether access differs by income, education, race, disability, or vehicle availability.

Torrance application. This separate section cites the Torrance General Plan and Torrance Transit Tomorrow Plan. Torrance has major employment, residential, commercial, and industrial areas, while its transit routes extend into neighboring jurisdictions. A local accessibility analysis should not stop at the city boundary.

A conceptual study could map jobs reachable within selected travel times by car, transit, walking, and bicycling. It should use service by time of day, account for transfers and first-and-last-mile conditions, and distinguish job categories where possible. Housing cost and worker characteristics would help reveal whether proximity translates into a realistic match.

Scenario comparisons could test a land-use change, a frequency improvement, or a safer connection. The result should report both regional access and local burdens such as truck conflict, displacement risk, or increased exposure.

No such model is calculated here. The application shows why urban form, service, and the labor market must be interpreted together rather than reduced to a density target.
