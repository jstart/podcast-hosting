## Transcript

Every California city is required to have a General Plan, sometimes called the city's constitution for land use. It's a long-range policy document covering where housing can go, where businesses can go, how traffic should flow, and increasingly, thanks to state mandates, how the city plans to actually meet its share of regional housing need. State law requires several mandatory elements in every general plan: land use, circulation, housing, conservation, open space, noise, safety, and, added more recently, environmental justice. The general plan sets the vision. The zoning code is what actually implements it, dividing the city into specific zones, residential, commercial, industrial, mixed-use, each with its own rules about what can be built and how tall, how dense, how far back from the street.

When someone wants to build something that doesn't automatically fit the zoning code as written, a conditional use permit, a variance, a larger development, that proposal typically goes first to city staff for review, and from there to the Planning Commission, an appointed body of residents, not elected officials, who hold public hearings and vote on whether to approve it. Planning commissioners are appointed by the city council, but once seated they operate somewhat independently, and their decisions can usually be appealed up to the city council itself if someone, whether the applicant or a neighbor, disagrees with the outcome.

Layered on top of all of this is the California Environmental Quality Act, CEQA, which requires many projects to undergo formal environmental review before they can be approved. CEQA is one of the most fought-over laws in California politics. Supporters see it as essential protection against pollution, traffic, and environmental harm. Critics argue it's frequently weaponized, not to protect the environment, but to slow down or kill housing and infrastructure projects that opponents simply don't want built, regardless of their actual environmental impact. Both things can be true in different cases, which is exactly why CEQA fights show up in local news constantly.

Something important has shifted in recent years: the state has been steadily taking zoning authority away from cities, especially around housing. Laws like SB 9 now require most cities to allow duplexes and lot splits on land that used to be zoned strictly for single-family homes, regardless of what the local zoning code says. SB 35 lets developers bypass much of the normal discretionary review process entirely if a city has fallen behind on its state-mandated housing targets. Every city, Torrance included, is required to adopt a Housing Element as part of its general plan, essentially a state-audited promise about how many housing units it will zone for, at what affordability levels, over the coming years. Fall short, and the state has real tools to force compliance. Local control over land use, once close to absolute, is now genuinely shared with Sacramento.

This is also, not coincidentally, one of the most common subjects covered in the "citizens academy" programs that some California cities run for residents, walking attendees through exactly how a project moves from an application on a planner's desk to a shovel in the ground. If your own city offers one, it's usually the single best way to actually watch this process happen in real time rather than just reading about it.
For more, see Torrance's Community Development Department, Planning Division, and its published Planning Commission agendas at torranceca.gov.
