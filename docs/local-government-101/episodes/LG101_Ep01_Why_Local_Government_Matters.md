## Transcript

Most people can name the President. Fewer can name their county supervisor. Almost nobody can name their water district board member. That's backwards, because the closer a government sits to you, the more it actually touches your life day to day.

Local government isn't one thing. It's four overlapping layers stacked on top of each other in the same physical space: your city, your county, a scattering of special districts you've probably never heard of, and your school district. Each one has its own elected officials, its own budget, and its own slice of what happens on your street. A pothole is the city's problem. A trial is the county's. Your tap water might belong to a special district entirely separate from both. And who's teaching your kids answers to a school board that has nothing to do with the mayor.

Take Torrance as the example running through this series. It's one of the largest cities in the South Bay, home to roughly a hundred and forty thousand people, and it's unusual for a modern California city in one specific way: it runs almost everything itself. Its own police department. Its own fire department. Its own water utility. Its own bus system, Torrance Transit. Most of its South Bay neighbors contract at least some of these out, usually to Los Angeles County. Torrance mostly doesn't. That makes it a genuinely useful place to study, because so much of what a city can choose to do or not do is visible right there in its own departments.

Torrance doesn't operate alone, though. It sits inside Los Angeles County's 4th Supervisorial District, a district so large it covers more than thirty cities and over two million residents, stretching from the Palos Verdes Peninsula through Torrance and down into Long Beach and the Gateway Cities. It's also one of sixteen member cities in the South Bay Cities Council of Governments, a regional body that coordinates things no single city can solve alone, like transportation funding, water conservation, and homelessness services. And within its own borders, or close to them, sit school districts, water agencies, and sanitation districts, each with legal authority that the city government simply doesn't have.

Why should any of this matter to you? Because turnout in local elections is often a small fraction of turnout in presidential ones, which means the votes that do get cast carry outsized weight. Because the decisions that shape your actual daily experience, whether a road gets repaved, whether a housing project gets approved next door, whether your kid's school gets a new librarian, are almost never made in Washington or even Sacramento. They're made a few miles from where you're listening to this, often by people who would happily meet with you if you showed up.

Over the next nine episodes, we'll take this apart piece by piece: how city hall actually works, what the county does that the city can't, the invisible special districts that run your water and your buses, who decides what gets built next door, where the money comes from, how school districts fit into all of this, why your local vote matters more than you think, and finally, concretely, how to get involved. None of it requires becoming a policy expert. It just requires knowing where to look.
For more, see the City of Torrance's own government pages at torranceca.gov, and the South Bay Cities Council of Governments at southbaycities.org.
