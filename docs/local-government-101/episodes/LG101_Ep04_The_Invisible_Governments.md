## Transcript

California has more than four thousand special districts, and most residents couldn't name a single one they live inside. A special district is a local government built to do exactly one thing, usually something that doesn't respect city boundaries: move water, treat sewage, put out fires, run a cemetery, manage a library system. Some are run by their own elected boards. Others are governed by boards appointed from the cities they serve. Almost all of them can raise money through fees or taxes, and almost all of them operate below most residents' radar entirely.

Here's where Torrance is genuinely unusual. It runs its own water utility as a city department, not through an outside special district, which is not the norm for the region. But Torrance still depends on a special district for its water supply in the first place: West Basin Municipal Water District, a wholesale agency that buys imported water and sells it to the retail water providers across the South Bay, including Torrance's own water department. So even a city that runs its own retail utility still answers to a regional wholesale district behind the scenes.

Wastewater tells a similar story. Once water goes down the drain, it typically becomes the job of the County Sanitation Districts of Los Angeles County, a set of special districts built specifically because sewage treatment requires regional infrastructure, treatment plants and trunk lines, that no single city could reasonably build and maintain alone.

Fire protection is where Torrance's independence really stands out. Many South Bay cities contract their fire and emergency medical service out to the Los Angeles County Fire Department rather than running their own. Torrance instead maintains its own city fire department, with its own stations and its own history dating back roughly a century. That's a real, meaningful choice: local control and direct accountability to Torrance's own city council, in exchange for the cost of running the whole operation independently rather than sharing costs regionally.

Transit is the same story again. Los Angeles County Metro runs most of the region's buses and trains. But Torrance operates Torrance Transit, its own municipal bus system, a genuine rarity in a county this size. It's a small, concrete example of the same underlying question every South Bay city keeps answering differently: run it yourself, or hand it to someone bigger.

And then there's a special district hiding in plain sight that gets its own full episode later in this series: your school district. Legally, a school district is its own independent local education agency, governed by its own elected board, with its own budget and its own taxing authority, entirely separate from the city government even though it sits inside the same city limits.

The common thread across all of these: special districts exist because some problems, water supply, sewage treatment, fire coverage across a whole region, are more efficient to solve at a scale bigger than one city, but the boards making those decisions are elected in low-visibility, low-turnout races that most residents never think about. That combination, real power plus almost no scrutiny, is exactly why they're sometimes called the shadow governments of California.
For more, see West Basin Municipal Water District at westbasin.org, and the Torrance Fire Department's own history page at torranceca.gov.
