## Transcript

Here's a mix-up worth clearing up early: your city council does not run your local schools. A school district is its own independent local education agency, legally separate from the city government, with its own elected board, its own budget, and its own boundaries, which don't necessarily match the city's boundaries at all. Torrance Unified School District serves roughly twenty-two thousand students across thirty-two schools, including four comprehensive high schools, spread across about twenty-one square miles, and it answers to its own five-member Board of Education, not to the Torrance City Council.

That board is elected by trustee area, meaning the district is divided into geographic zones and each trustee represents residents from a specific area, similar in spirit to how Torrance's own city council shifted from citywide, at-large seats to district-based ones. It's the same underlying legal pressure driving both changes: the California Voting Rights Act, which has pushed hundreds of California cities and school districts toward geographic representation over the last decade, on the theory that district-based elections give concentrated communities a real chance to elect a candidate of their choosing, something citywide at-large elections can make much harder.

Where does a school district's money actually come from? Mostly the state, through a formula called the Local Control Funding Formula, which sends a base amount per student plus extra funding for students who are low-income, English learners, or in foster care. Property tax revenue collected locally also flows into the mix, but the state effectively backstops and redistributes school funding statewide, which is a large part of why school funding debates constantly circle back to Sacramento rather than city hall, even though schools are about as local as government gets.

Even though the school district and the city government are legally separate, they run into each other constantly. Construction near a school needs city permits and often city fire and police coordination. Traffic and safety around drop-off and pick-up times are frequently a joint city-and-district problem, sometimes worked out through shared crossing-guard programs or coordinated traffic engineering. Many districts and cities sign joint-use agreements, letting the public use school athletic fields after hours, or letting city recreation programs run out of school gyms, in exchange for the city helping maintain those facilities. And a school resource officer, when a district has one, is typically a city police officer assigned to work with the district, funded through some blend of both budgets.

None of this overlap changes the basic fact that they're two entirely separate governments, elected separately, funded separately, and accountable to two different boards that may not always agree with each other, or with the city council down the street. If you want a deeper, dedicated look at how California school funding, curriculum, and accountability actually work, that's the entire subject of the companion podcast in this series, How California Schools Work.
