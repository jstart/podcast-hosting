## Transcript

Local elections routinely draw a fraction of the turnout that a presidential race pulls in. A city council seat, a school board trustee area, a water district board, these are sometimes decided by a few thousand votes, occasionally even a few hundred. Do the arithmetic on that, and a single vote in a local race carries dramatically more statistical weight than a single vote in a national one. Most people intuitively feel the opposite is true, that local races are too small to matter. The actual math says the reverse.

Torrance itself is a live example of how much local election structures have changed recently, and why. For most of its history, all of Torrance's city council seats, like most California cities, were elected citywide, at-large: every voter in the city could vote for every seat. Over roughly the last decade, hundreds of California cities and school districts, Torrance and Torrance Unified among them, shifted away from that model toward district-based elections, where each seat represents a specific geographic slice of the city or district instead.

The legal engine behind that wave of change is the California Voting Rights Act. Unlike its federal counterpart, it doesn't require proving intentional discrimination, only that an at-large election system results in "racially polarized voting" that dilutes a minority group's ability to elect a candidate of its choice. That's a comparatively low bar to clear in court, and cities that lose these lawsuits, or simply want to avoid the legal costs of fighting one, often owe the challenger's attorney fees on top of everything else. Facing that risk, most California cities have simply switched to district elections voluntarily rather than litigate it out, which is exactly what happened here.

There's an ongoing debate about whether that shift actually works as intended. Supporters argue district elections make it easier for candidates without citywide name recognition or citywide fundraising networks to win, and give geographically concentrated communities real representation they didn't have before. Critics argue that dividing a small city into six or seven tiny districts can just as easily fragment political power and make it harder to build citywide coalitions, without necessarily changing who actually turns out to vote. Both arguments show up in real academic research, and the honest answer, so far, is that results have varied significantly from city to city.

Separately, California has also been pushing many cities toward holding their elections in even-numbered years, aligned with statewide and federal elections, rather than in odd years on their own. The reasoning is blunt: turnout in even-year elections, when a governor's race or presidential race is also on the ballot, runs dramatically higher than in standalone odd-year local elections, sometimes by a factor of two or three. Torrance already holds its elections in even years. Plenty of California cities and districts didn't, until relatively recent state law forced the issue.

The practical upshot: your sample ballot in an even-numbered year is going to be long, city council or mayor, school board trustees, water district seats, county measures, state propositions, all stacked on top of each other. It's easy to vote at the top of the ballot and stop. Don't. The races near the bottom are usually the ones where your vote is mathematically most likely to matter.
For more, see your county registrar-recorder's sample ballot before each election, and Ballotpedia's coverage of local races in your area.
