## Transcript

Zoom out from Torrance and you land in Los Angeles County, the single largest county in the entire United States, home to roughly ten million people. If it were a state, it would be more populous than more than three dozen actual states. And yet it's governed by just five people: the Los Angeles County Board of Supervisors. Do the math on that and each supervisor represents something like two million residents. For years, critics have nicknamed the board "five little kings," because so few elected officials wield so much authority over so many people, with so little day-to-day media scrutiny compared to, say, the mayor of a major city.

Torrance sits in the 4th Supervisorial District, a sprawling stretch that runs from the Palos Verdes Peninsula through Torrance, down through Long Beach, and into the Gateway Cities, covering more than thirty cities and a couple dozen unincorporated communities in total. Whoever holds that seat has a real say over services touching a huge swath of the South Bay, even though most residents there would struggle to name them.

So what does a county actually do that a city doesn't? A lot of it is the unglamorous, expensive machinery of daily life that cities were never built to handle. Courts and jails. Public health departments and hospitals. Social services: welfare programs, foster care, child protective services, In-Home Supportive Services for elderly and disabled residents. The county assessor determines property values for tax purposes for every parcel in the county, city or no city. And the county's registrar-recorder actually runs elections for the cities inside it, including Torrance's own city council races, even though the county has no say over who wins them.

There's a category distinction worth understanding here: incorporated versus unincorporated. Torrance is incorporated, meaning it has its own city government providing municipal services. But large parts of Los Angeles County aren't incorporated into any city at all. In those unincorporated areas, the county itself acts as the city government, providing everything from law enforcement, through the Sheriff's Department, to planning and zoning decisions, to fire protection. If you've ever driven through a neighborhood that felt like a city but technically wasn't one, that's likely unincorporated county territory.

The county and a city like Torrance interact constantly without most residents noticing. County public health inspects Torrance restaurants. The county courts handle Torrance's criminal cases and civil disputes, even though Torrance has its own police department making the arrests. County social workers manage foster care placements for Torrance families. And regional infrastructure, flood control channels, county-run parks, and County Sanitation Districts, all cross city lines freely because water, sewage, and traffic never respected a municipal boundary in the first place.

The practical takeaway: if a problem is really about crime enforcement or a pothole, that's city hall. If it's about the courts, welfare benefits, public health, or anything happening in an unincorporated pocket nearby, that's the county, and specifically, for a huge chunk of the South Bay, that's the office of your 4th District Supervisor.
For more, see the Los Angeles County Board of Supervisors' own pages at lacounty.gov, including the 4th District's page describing its cities and communities.
