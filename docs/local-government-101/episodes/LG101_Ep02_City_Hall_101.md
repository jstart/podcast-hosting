## Transcript

Every California city is either a general law city, which just follows the powers the state legislature hands out by default, or a charter city, which has written its own local constitution granting itself additional authority over what are called "municipal affairs." Torrance is a charter city, with a charter its voters ratified in 1946, which gives it more control than a general law city would have over things like how its elections are run and how its own contracts are handled.

Most California cities, charter or not, use what's called the council-manager form of government, and Torrance is one of them. The idea is a deliberate split of labor. The city council is the elected, political side: it sets policy, adopts the budget, passes ordinances, and makes the big land-use calls. The city manager is the professional, appointed side: a career administrator, hired by the council, who runs the actual departments day to day, supervises staff, and carries out whatever the council has decided. It's the same logic as a corporate board versus a CEO. The council decides where the city is going. The city manager drives.

Torrance's council has seven members: six represent individual geographic districts, and one, the mayor, is elected citywide. All seven serve four-year terms, capped at two terms each, with elections held in even-numbered years. That district structure is relatively recent. Like hundreds of California cities over the last decade, Torrance shifted away from electing all its council members citywide, at-large, and moved toward geographic districts instead. That shift usually happens under pressure from the California Voting Rights Act, which makes at-large elections legally vulnerable if they dilute the voting power of a minority group that could otherwise elect a candidate of its choice from a smaller district. We'll come back to that fight in a later episode, because it reshaped local elections across the entire state.

The mayor's job is mostly about running meetings and representing the city publicly, signing contracts and official documents, showing up at ribbon cuttings, but casting one vote on the council just like everyone else. There's no strong-mayor system here, no separate executive branch the way a governor or a president has. Some California cities, notably Los Angeles, San Diego, and San Francisco, do use a mayor-council system with a genuinely separate, powerful executive. Torrance, like the large majority of California cities, doesn't.

Two more elected officials round out Torrance's structure: a city clerk, who manages elections and public records, and a city treasurer, who oversees the city's finances and investments. Both are directly elected, not appointed, which is itself a choice some cities make differently. A separately appointed city attorney handles the legal work.

None of these seven council members, or the clerk, or the treasurer, actually run a department themselves. That's the whole point of the council-manager system: professional staff run operations, so that day-to-day city services don't swing wildly every time a new council majority takes office. What does swing with elections is direction. Priorities. Which streets get resurfaced first, whether a new park gets built, how aggressively the city pursues affordable housing. That's what a city council election actually decides.
For more, see Torrance's own "About Your City Government" page at torranceca.gov, and the City Council Districts page describing how district boundaries were established.
