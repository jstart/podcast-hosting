## Transcript

A transportation mode is a method for moving people or goods, supported by infrastructure, vehicles, operators, rules, and energy. The source chapter ranges from walking, cycling, automobiles, buses, and rail to freight trucks, ships, aircraft, pipelines, and other specialized systems. No mode is best for every trip. Distance, volume, speed, cost, reliability, accessibility, safety, emissions, and the available network all shape suitability.

Multimodal and intermodal are related but distinct. A multimodal system offers more than one way to travel. An intermodal trip connects modes through coordinated facilities, information, payment, or operations. A bicycle carried on a bus is an intermodal connection. Freight transferred in a standard container among ship, rail, and truck is another.

The complete trip matters. A fast line-haul service is not accessible if a passenger cannot safely reach the stop. Transfers impose time and uncertainty. Freight terminals create regional benefits and local truck, noise, and land-use effects. Planning therefore evaluates the network, not only the vehicle.

Mode choice also reflects public investment. Parking supply, street design, service frequency, pricing, and land-use location change the relative convenience of each option. Calling those results individual preference can hide the structure that produced them.

Torrance application. This local section is separate from the textbook adaptation and cites the Torrance Transit Tomorrow Plan and the City's Multi-Use Active Transportation Trail project page. The transit plan describes a bus network extending beyond Torrance into neighboring jurisdictions. The trail project describes a proposed active-transportation connection between the Regional Transit Center and Downtown Torrance.

Together, those sources support an intermodal question: can a person walk, roll, or bicycle to the transit center, make a reliable regional connection, and complete the return trip at the needed hour? A review would examine crossings, wayfinding, bicycle parking or carriage, transfer time, lighting, disability access, fare information, and first-and-last-mile gaps.

For freight, the same network perspective would identify truck routes, industrial access, curb conflicts, pavement needs, and exposure near homes or schools. Passenger and goods movement cannot be evaluated as if they use separate cities.

This exercise does not claim that a proposed Torrance project resolves every connection. It demonstrates how chapter five's mode framework turns into a complete-trip audit.
