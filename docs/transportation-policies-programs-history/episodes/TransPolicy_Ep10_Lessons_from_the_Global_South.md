## Transcript

The term Global South covers diverse places rather than one transportation condition. The source chapter examines rapidly urbanizing cities across Africa, Asia, and Latin America while warning against treating them as a single model. Population growth, uneven development, limited public resources, informality, colonial histories, and climate risk shape mobility in different ways.

Walking, cycling, motorcycles, minibuses, shared taxis, and other formal and informal services often carry a large share of trips. Informal transport can provide flexible routes, employment, and first-and-last-mile coverage where formal networks do not reach. It can also present safety, accessibility, labor, harassment, congestion, and regulatory challenges. Policy should understand the service it provides before attempting to replace or formalize it.

Bus rapid transit has been an influential innovation. Dedicated right-of-way, stations, frequent service, fare collection, and network design can deliver high capacity at lower capital cost than some rail systems. Outcomes depend on corridor conditions, operations, integration, affordability, and the treatment of existing operators and riders.

The chapter's strongest lesson is reciprocal learning. Cities in the Global North should not extract a visible technology and ignore the institutions and local knowledge that made it work. A policy must fit governance, street space, demand, culture, resources, and equity.

Torrance application. This comparison is separate from the textbook adaptation and cites the Torrance Transit Tomorrow Plan. Torrance is not presented as equivalent to any Global South city. The local source describes a municipal bus system operating across multiple South Bay jurisdictions and a plan focused on rider experience, ridership, service design, regional connections, and the Regional Transit Center.

The transferable method is to start with actual travel patterns and existing services. Before importing a branded solution, Torrance planners could ask which trips are underserved, where transfers fail, which riders face the highest cost, and which operating changes fit local streets and institutions. A bus-priority treatment should be evaluated through reliability, person throughput, safety, access, and effects on connecting service.

Local adaptation also means involving operators and riders early. They often know delay points and practical barriers that a regional average hides.

This segment does not recommend bus rapid transit or any specific Torrance change. It demonstrates respectful comparative learning without claiming that contexts are interchangeable.
