## Transcript

You have been asked to recommend an improvement to a California city's online permit portal. Your reader is a busy council member who has heard confident software promises before. A useful response needs more than polished sentences. It needs a debatable thesis, evidence the reader can inspect, fair treatment of limits, and a recommendation narrow enough to act on.

Begin with the rhetorical situation. Every piece of writing occurs among an author, an audience, a purpose, a subject, and a context. Here, you are the analyst. The audience is a council member and the public. The purpose is to support a decision. The subject is access to a permit service. The context includes budget limits, legal duties, public records, language access, and residents who may not use the portal.

Those conditions shape the document. A technical report for software staff can discuss error logs in detail. A council memo should explain what the errors mean for service, cost, and accountability. Writing for an audience does not mean telling readers only what they want to hear. It means giving them the information and structure they need to judge the claim.

A thesis is the main claim. In an argument, it should be debatable, specific, and supportable. Consider this sentence: the portal should be improved. Almost nobody can meaningfully disagree because the word improved says little. Now try this: the city should fund a ninety-day repair of the Spanish-language upload path, keep Thursday counter hours during the test, and publish completion rates by language before deciding on a wider portal contract. That claim identifies an action, a limit, a measurement, and a later decision.

Narrowing strengthens a thesis. A claim about all digital access would require evidence about many services and populations. A claim about one documented failure point can be supported with focused evidence. Qualifiers also matter. Words such as likely, generally, or under these conditions can make a claim more accurate. A qualified claim is not automatically weak. It shows that the writer knows where the evidence stops.

An argument connects claims, reasons, and evidence. Evidence might include a dated city audit, portal completion data, user interviews, a statute, a budget estimate, or a study of a comparable service. Evidence does not speak for itself. The writer must explain the connection. If forty percent of sessions end at document upload, that figure supports a repair only after the reader knows the denominator, time period, language path, comparison rate, and whether ending a session means failure.

The bridge between evidence and claim is sometimes called a warrant. If the recommendation is to preserve counter hours, the warrant may be that an online repair will not serve residents who lack reliable internet, accessible technology, required documents, or confidence in the system. State that reasoning. Otherwise, readers may connect the same facts differently.

A strong argument also handles counterarguments. A vendor may favor replacing the whole portal. A budget officer may question incomplete data. Present those concerns fairly, then answer them with evidence.

Source evaluation asks whether a source deserves the job you give it. Start with relevance. Does it address a comparable service, population, place, and period? Then ask about authority. Who created it, and what expertise or institutional role do they have? Examine evidence and method. Can you tell how the data were collected? Look at currency. Has the technology or law changed? Consider purpose and bias. Is the source informing, selling, advocating, or entertaining?

Bias does not make a source useless, but it changes how you use it. A vendor case study may select favorable results. A city audit may have a narrow compliance focus. A resident survey may miss people who never received it. Label each source's contribution and limitation.

Cross-check important facts. If a summary cites a city report, open the report. If a statistic appears without a date or denominator, do not build the thesis on it. Distinguish primary sources, such as the audit or ordinance itself, from later commentary. Secondary sources can provide interpretation and context, but citing the original public record lets the reader verify the claim.

Integrate sources through quotation, paraphrase, and summary. Quotation repeats exact language and requires quotation marks plus citation. Paraphrase restates a specific passage in genuinely new wording and structure, with citation. Summary condenses a broader source, also with citation. Changing a few words does not turn copied language into a paraphrase.

Citation gives credit and lets readers locate evidence. In-text references and a works-cited or reference list must correspond. The required details depend on the assigned style, such as MLA, APA, or Chicago. Consistency matters, but citation is not merely punctuation. Introduce the source, identify what it contributes, and explain how it supports the argument. A citation after an unsupported leap does not repair the leap.

Standard written English helps readers follow the reasoning. Check sentence boundaries, subject-verb agreement, pronoun reference, verb tense, modifiers, punctuation, and word choice. A fragment may leave a reason unfinished. A comma splice may blur two claims. An unclear pronoun can make the reader wonder which agency acted. Grammar supports meaning.

Prefer concrete language. The sentence, resident access was meaningfully optimized, hides the actor and the result. The sentence, the planning department added a phone appointment option and completed thirty more applications in June, names both. Active voice often helps, but passive voice can be appropriate when the actor is unknown or the receiver matters most. Choose deliberately.

Revision is larger than proofreading. First revise the document. Does the thesis answer the assignment? Is the organization appropriate for the audience and purpose? Does every major section help establish the claim? Move, add, or delete whole sections before polishing individual sentences.

Next revise paragraphs. Give each paragraph a clear focus. Use transitions that show cause, contrast, example, or limitation. Keep evidence near the claim it supports and follow it with explanation.

Then revise sentences. Cut repetition, vague nouns, inflated adjectives, and empty openings. Vary sentence length when it improves rhythm and emphasis. Read aloud to hear missing words and overloaded clauses. Finally, edit and proofread for correctness, citations, names, dates, formatting, and typographical errors.

Practice on a weak draft: the revolutionary new portal will dramatically improve access for all residents. The sentence overclaims, supplies no evidence, and ignores residents who cannot use the portal. A better version is this: if upload errors explain the lower completion rate on the Spanish-language path, repairing that step should increase completed applications during the ninety-day test. The city will publish the before and after rates and keep Thursday counter hours while it evaluates the result.

The revision improves the argument in five ways. It makes the condition explicit. It names the affected path. It proposes a measurement period. It preserves a non-digital option. It promises evidence before a larger claim. Notice that it does not promise success. It proposes a test.

For a California local-government application, organize the memo around a decision. Open with the thesis. Describe the documented problem and scope. Present two credible sources, including one primary city source. Explain the warrant connecting the evidence to the recommendation. Address cost, legal or access constraints, and a reasonable counterargument. End with an action the council can vote on, a report-back date, and a measurement.

The public context raises another writing duty. Do not hide uncertainty. If the city does not know why sessions end at upload, say the data show correlation, not cause. Recommend interviews or usability testing before claiming a diagnosis. If language data exclude users who did not select a language, name that gap. Precision protects both the argument and the residents described by it.

Three recall questions.

First, what makes an argumentative thesis useful?

Second, what questions help evaluate a source?

Third, how is revision different from proofreading?

First answer: a useful thesis is debatable, specific, narrow enough to support, and responsive to its audience, purpose, and context.

Second answer: ask about relevance, authority, evidence and method, currency, purpose, bias, and whether important facts can be cross-checked.

Third answer: revision rethinks claims, evidence, organization, paragraphs, and sentences. Proofreading is the later check for surface errors and formatting.

Artifact: write a five-hundred to seven-hundred-fifty-word policy argument about one California local service. Include a qualified thesis, two sources the reader can open, an explanation connecting evidence to the claim, one counterargument, one evidence limit, one properly matched citation and reference entry, and a recommendation with a report-back date. Revise once at the document level and once at the sentence level.
