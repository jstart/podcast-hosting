A California city wants a digital permit portal. Residents should be able to submit plans, pay fees, and track a review. Who has the authority to set the rules: the city, California, or the national government? Before choosing software, a public servant needs a constitutional map.

Begin with the problem the Constitution tried to solve. Under the Articles of Confederation, the national government depended heavily on the states. Congress could not reliably raise revenue through direct taxation, regulate trade among the states, or force states to follow national decisions. The arrangement protected state independence, but it made common action difficult. Economic disputes, debt, and unrest exposed the weakness of a union whose center could request more easily than it could govern.

The Constitutional Convention produced a different design. The Constitution created a national government with its own authority over individuals, while preserving states as governments with substantial powers. That design did not simply make the center stronger. It divided national power among a legislature, an executive, and a judiciary. It also divided the legislature into a House of Representatives and a Senate.

These choices grew from disagreement. Large states favored representation based on population. Small states feared permanent disadvantage. The Great Compromise gave each state equal representation in the Senate and representation based on population in the House. Other compromises protected slavery and increased the political influence of slaveholding states. The Three-Fifths Compromise counted three out of every five enslaved people for representation and direct taxation, even though enslaved people had no political rights. The Constitution therefore combined durable governing ideas with profound injustice.

Ratification produced another debate. Federalists argued that a stronger union could govern a large republic, control faction through competition among interests, and provide order without concentrating all power in one place. Anti-Federalists worried that the proposed national government was distant and powerful, and that the document lacked an explicit protection of individual liberties. The promise of amendments helped secure ratification. The first ten amendments became the Bill of Rights.

The Constitution is brief because it establishes a framework. Article One creates Congress. Article Two creates the presidency. Article Three creates the federal judiciary. Separation of powers assigns different functions to those branches. Checks and balances give each branch tools that affect the others. Congress passes a bill, the president may veto it, and Congress may override that veto with the required supermajority. The Senate confirms many appointments. Courts review disputes about law and constitutional authority. Congress controls appropriations and may impeach and remove federal officers through separate House and Senate actions.

Do not confuse that horizontal division with federalism. Separation of powers divides authority among branches at one level. Federalism divides authority between the national government and state governments. A state legislature and Congress are not two branches. They are lawmaking institutions in different governments.

The national government has enumerated powers, meaning powers stated in the Constitution. Congress may tax and spend, regulate commerce among the states and with foreign nations, coin money, establish post offices, and raise armed forces. The Necessary and Proper Clause supports implied powers that reasonably carry enumerated powers into effect. The Supremacy Clause makes the Constitution, valid federal laws, and treaties the supreme law of the land when state and federal rules conflict.

States retain broad reserved powers through the constitutional structure and the Tenth Amendment. These include much of the police power used to protect health, safety, and welfare. States organize elections, license professions, structure local governments, and take primary responsibility for public education. Some powers are concurrent. Both national and state governments tax, borrow, spend, make laws, and operate courts.

Local governments occupy a different position. The Constitution divides power between the nation and the states, but cities and counties are created under state law. A California city exercises authority granted or recognized by California's constitution and statutes. Its council may adopt ordinances within that authority. Its staff may administer zoning and permitting. State law can also limit what the city may do.

The balance of federalism has changed over time. Dual federalism describes a model in which national and state responsibilities are imagined as fairly separate. Cooperative federalism describes overlapping work, with multiple levels funding and administering the same policy area. Modern transportation, environmental, emergency, education, and health programs often use this shared model.

Money is one of the national government's strongest tools. A categorical grant supports a defined purpose and often carries detailed conditions. A block grant gives recipients more discretion within a broader area. A mandate requires action, while an unfunded mandate places duties on state or local government without supplying all the money needed to perform them. These distinctions matter because a city may experience federalism as a technical requirement attached to funds, not as a dramatic courtroom conflict.

Federalism offers potential benefits. States can respond to regional preferences, try different policies, and provide more points of access for participation. It also creates costs. Services and rights can vary by location. Overlapping authority can confuse accountability. A policy innovation in one state may help residents, while another state's policy may deepen inequality. Competition among governments does not guarantee a fair result.

Now apply the map to the permit portal.

The city chooses many operational details. It can select a vendor under its procurement rules, arrange the workflow, train staff, and decide how the status page looks. It can set some local fees and procedures when state law permits. But local choice sits inside higher-level requirements.

California may define building standards, housing review timelines, public records duties, privacy requirements, and the legal authority of cities. A city cannot use a local workflow to cancel a state deadline. The national government may set civil rights and accessibility duties that affect digital public services. Federal grant money may add security, reporting, or procurement conditions. The county may maintain parcel or tax records that the city portal needs, even when county staff do not control the portal.

Suppose the project team wants one field asking for disability information so it can offer assistance. That helpful idea raises privacy, access, and records questions. Suppose the council wants neighborhood-level data about delayed housing applications. State law may determine the deadline, city staff may calculate the measure, and federal or state privacy law may constrain publication. One dashboard feature can involve all three levels.

The practical method is to trace each requirement to an authority. Label it federal, state, county, or city. Then label the mechanism: constitutional rule, statute, regulation, grant condition, ordinance, contract, or internal procedure. Finally, identify who can change it. City staff can revise a procedure. A council may change an ordinance. Neither can repeal a state statute or waive a valid federal requirement.

This approach also clarifies accountability. If the portal is slow because the city selected poor software, blaming federalism is evasive. If a required review exists in state law, promising that a new app will erase it is misleading. Technology can simplify administration, but it cannot silently move constitutional authority.

Here are three recall questions. Pause after the questions and answer in your own words.

First, how does federalism differ from separation of powers?

Second, what is the relationship between enumerated and implied national powers?

Third, why might a grant influence a city policy even when the national government does not administer the local program?

First, federalism divides authority between the national government and the states. Separation of powers divides functions among legislative, executive, and judicial branches within a government.

Second, enumerated powers are listed in the Constitution. Implied powers are means used to carry those listed powers into effect, supported by provisions such as the Necessary and Proper Clause.

Third, grant funding can carry conditions. A state or city that accepts the money may need to follow national standards, reporting rules, or program limits while local officials perform the work.
