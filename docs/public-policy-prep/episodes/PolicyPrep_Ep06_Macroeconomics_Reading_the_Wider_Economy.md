## Transcript

A California county sees rental-assistance requests rise while sales-tax revenue weakens. Need is increasing just as the budget feels tighter. Those facts are not contradictory. They may be two local signs of the same economic slowdown. Macroeconomics provides a disciplined way to connect national output, jobs, prices, and spending to a local government's choices without pretending city hall controls the national economy.

This is preparation only. No college credit or transfer credit follows from this episode.

Gross domestic product, or GDP, is the market value of final goods and services produced within a country during a period. Final prevents double counting. The value of flour sold to a bakery is already embodied in the bread sold to a customer. Production within the country counts regardless of the producer's nationality.

Economists calculate GDP through spending as consumption plus investment plus government purchases plus exports minus imports. Investment includes business equipment, structures, housing construction, and inventories, not stock purchases. Transfer payments are not direct purchases of current production. Imports are subtracted because they were produced elsewhere.

Nominal GDP values output at current prices. Real GDP uses constant prices to separate changes in production from changes in the price level. The GDP deflator is a broad price index derived from nominal and real GDP. Real GDP per person helps compare average material output across times or places, but it is not a complete measure of well-being.

GDP poorly captures unpaid labor, leisure, underground activity, environmental damage, and income distribution. Rebuilding after disaster can raise production while people remain worse off. GDP remains useful, but only for the question it measures.

Economic growth is a sustained increase in real GDP per person. Labor productivity, or output per hour, rises through physical capital, human capital, technology, infrastructure, and effective institutions. Capital faces diminishing returns when other inputs are fixed, while technology can expand productive capacity. Compound growth makes small annual differences important over decades. Growth can raise living standards while still creating pollution or unequal gains.

The unemployment rate equals unemployed people divided by the labor force. To count as unemployed, a person must generally be without a job, available for work, and actively seeking work. The labor force includes employed and unemployed people. It excludes many people who are not seeking work, such as some students, retirees, caregivers, and discouraged workers.

The labor force participation rate compares the labor force with the working-age population. If people stop searching, they leave the measured labor force, so unemployment can fall without new jobs. Frictional unemployment comes from job transitions. Structural unemployment reflects mismatch in skills or location. Cyclical unemployment rises in downturns. The natural rate includes frictional and structural unemployment, not zero unemployment.

Inflation is a continuing increase in the overall price level, not merely a rise in one price. The consumer price index, or CPI, tracks the cost of a representative basket purchased by consumers. The inflation rate is the percentage change in a price index. Because buying patterns and quality change, no index is perfect.

Inflation reduces purchasing power when income does not keep pace and redistributes between borrowers and lenders. Deflation is a falling price level. Disinflation means inflation remains positive but slows. If wages rise four percent while prices rise six percent, real purchasing power falls roughly two percent. The real interest rate is approximately the nominal rate minus expected inflation.

Aggregate demand, or AD, represents planned consumption, investment, government purchases, and net exports at different price levels. Its curve slopes downward. Consumer confidence, taxes, wealth, debt, interest rates, business expectations, fiscal decisions, foreign income, and exchange rates can shift it. A fall in consumption or investment shifts AD left.

Short-run aggregate supply, or SRAS, commonly slopes upward because some wages and input prices adjust slowly. Costs, productivity, taxes, regulations, and expected inflation can shift it. A cost-driven shift left can raise prices while reducing output, called stagflation. Long-run aggregate supply reflects capacity based on labor, capital, technology, and institutions. A leftward AD shift can create a recessionary gap and cyclical unemployment. Excessive AD can create an inflationary gap.

The Keynesian model focuses on expenditure when prices and wages do not instantly restore full employment. Equilibrium occurs where planned expenditure equals output. Unexpected inventory growth leads firms to reduce production, while unexpected inventory loss leads them to increase it. The marginal propensity to consume, or MPC, is the share of added income spent. The marginal propensity to save, or MPS, is the share saved, and they sum to one. The simple multiplier is one divided by one minus the MPC. Taxes, imports, capacity, interest rates, expectations, and timing make real effects smaller or less predictable.

Fiscal policy uses spending and taxes to influence AD. Expansionary policy addresses recession, while contractionary policy addresses inflationary pressure. Automatic stabilizers change with the economy without a new vote. Policy also faces recognition, decision, implementation, and impact lags.

Apply the model carefully to a California local government. Cities and counties are not the federal government. They generally face balanced-budget constraints, cannot create national currency, and have limited control over aggregate demand. Their revenues also respond differently. Sales taxes can weaken quickly when households postpone vehicles, appliances, or restaurant spending. Property-tax revenue may move more slowly because assessment rules and billing cycles differ.

Suppose taxable sales fall four percent, unemployment rises, and applications for housing help increase. First separate nominal and real measures. If local prices rose, the decline in real taxable activity may be greater than the nominal decline suggests. Second separate indicators. A waitlist is not GDP, and county sales-tax receipts are not national consumption. They are local evidence consistent with a broader downturn.

The county should create a civic-tech dashboard with definitions, dates, and revision notes. Track taxable sales by broad category, benefit applications, processing time, unemployment and participation data, local job postings, CPI for the relevant region, and state or national real GDP. Show levels and rates of change. Do not mix monthly, quarterly, and annual series without labeling them.

The dashboard should explain lags. Unemployment may rise after spending slows, and sales-tax remittances arrive after transactions. Administrative data are timely but reflect eligibility and awareness, not all hardship.

For budgeting, build baseline, downside, and recovery scenarios. State the inflation assumption. Identify which contracts rise with a price index. Separate one-time reserves from ongoing revenue. Preserve high-value automatic responses where possible, while naming the opportunity cost of delaying a capital project or reducing another service.

A local government can make targeted purchases or accelerate maintenance, but its action is small relative to the national economy. The right claim is not that one county will end a recession. The claim is that it can understand the cycle, protect service delivery, avoid misleading nominal comparisons, and time local choices with greater care.

Three recall questions.

First, why does real GDP differ from nominal GDP?

Second, how can the unemployment rate fall without labor-market conditions improving?

Third, what happens in the AD-AS model when aggregate demand shifts left, all else equal?

First, nominal GDP uses current prices, while real GDP holds prices constant so changes better reflect the quantity of production.

Second, people who stop actively looking for work leave the measured labor force. They are no longer counted as unemployed, so the rate can fall even without new jobs.

Third, a leftward AD shift lowers short-run real output and the price level relative to what they otherwise would have been, creating cyclical unemployment and a possible recessionary gap.
