## Transcript

A California city moves residential parking permits online and announces that the new service is faster. Faster for whom? A resident with broadband, a credit card, stable housing, and flexible work hours may finish in five minutes. A resident sharing a phone, paid by the hour, or living at an address that does not match a vehicle record may face a very different system. That gap is both a civic problem and a sociology problem.

This is preparation only. Listening does not create college credit or transfer credit.

Sociology asks how individual lives connect to institutions and patterned relationships. Start with social stratification, the ranking of people and groups through unequal access to money, power, status, education, health, and security. Income is money received during a period. Wealth is the value of assets after debts. They are related but not interchangeable. Two households with similar paychecks may have very different abilities to survive a medical bill or a month without work.

Stratification can take forms such as caste and class. A class system allows some movement, but movement is not equally available. Social mobility can be upward, downward, or horizontal. A success story may show that movement is possible without proving that the structure is fair. Sociologists therefore compare life chances across groups, not just inspiring exceptions.

Global inequality extends the question across nations. Countries differ in income, infrastructure, health, education, and their position in world markets. National averages hide internal inequality. Modernization theory emphasizes industrial development. Dependency approaches emphasize colonial histories and unequal economic relationships. Each directs attention to different causes.

Race and ethnicity also shape institutions and outcomes. Race is a socially constructed system that assigns meaning to perceived physical differences. Ethnicity refers to shared cultural practices, ancestry, language, or identity. The fact that race is socially constructed does not mean its consequences are imaginary. Laws, housing markets, schools, and enforcement practices can produce durable effects from categories societies created. Prejudice concerns attitudes. Discrimination concerns action. Institutional discrimination appears when normal rules or routines create unequal results, even without an openly biased person at the counter.

The terms majority and minority describe power as well as numbers. A numerical majority can have less authority, and a numerical minority can control important resources. Stereotypes simplify whole groups into assumed traits. Scapegoating assigns blame to a less powerful group. Assimilation, pluralism, segregation, and expulsion describe very different relationships between groups. A civic analyst should not treat them as points on one harmless scale.

Gender is another social structure. Sex usually refers to biological characteristics, while gender refers to social meanings, identities, roles, and expectations. Gender socialization occurs through families, schools, peers, media, and workplaces. It can influence unpaid care, jobs, and whose behavior is judged as authoritative. Sexual orientation is distinct from gender identity. Intersectionality explains how class, race, gender, age, disability, and other positions combine.

Families are social institutions as well as personal relationships. Societies define kinship, marriage, parenting, and household membership in varied ways. A nuclear family includes parents and children, while an extended family includes other relatives. Families provide care, socialization, emotional support, and economic cooperation, but they can also contain conflict and unequal labor. Marriage rates, divorce, cohabitation, same-sex families, single-parent households, and chosen families all show that one household model cannot stand in for everyone.

Population study adds demographic tools. Fertility, mortality, and migration change the size and composition of a population. The crude birth rate and crude death rate count events relative to population size. Life expectancy summarizes expected years under current mortality conditions. Demographic transition theory describes a broad movement from high birth and death rates toward lower rates as societies industrialize. It is a model, not a timetable every country follows.

Urbanization is the growth of cities and the share of people living in urban areas. Cities concentrate opportunity, government, culture, and services, but also congestion, high land costs, displacement, and environmental burdens. Suburbanization and later movement back toward city centers can rearrange jobs and housing. Gentrification may bring investment while also raising rents and changing who can remain. An analyst should ask who receives the benefits, who bears the costs, and who had a voice.

Environmental sociology connects human organization to the natural world. Pollution, heat, flooding, and hazardous land uses are not distributed randomly. Environmental racism describes disproportionate environmental burdens placed on racialized communities. The tragedy of the commons explains how individually rational use of a shared resource can damage everyone when rules and accountability are weak. Sustainable policy tries to meet present needs without destroying future capacity.

Social change can be gradual or rapid. Technology, population shifts, environmental pressure, conflict, and social movements can all alter institutions. Collective behavior includes less structured group activity, while social movements pursue sustained change. Movements need grievances, but grievances alone are not enough. Resources, leadership, networks, political opportunities, and persuasive framing affect whether action grows. Reform movements seek limited change. Revolutionary movements seek a broad transformation. Resistance movements try to stop or reverse change.

Now return to the California parking portal. The city should examine the service as an institution, not merely as software. Eligibility rules, identity checks, payment methods, counter hours, language access, and enforcement all distribute benefits and burdens. A portal may be technically available while functionally unavailable to someone without a compatible device or stable documentation.

Build an equity review using evidence that local government can lawfully and responsibly collect. Measure starts, completions, abandonment points, processing time, appeal rates, and use of phone or counter channels. Break results down where privacy and sample size permit, such as language, neighborhood, device type, age band, or accommodation request. Do not infer race from a name or immigration status from a language. Do not publish tiny groups that could identify residents.

Family structure matters because a permit tied only to the leaseholder may exclude another adult who drives the household car. Gender matters when unpaid caregiving makes daytime appointments harder. Class matters when a fee or unpaid hour creates a larger sacrifice. Race and ethnicity may matter through residential patterns, language access, or prior experience with enforcement. Age and disability may affect device use or the need for an alternative process. Intersectionality warns that these barriers can compound.

Population and urban change matter too. A city with more multigenerational households may have more drivers than off-street spaces. New apartments may add residents under different parking rules than nearby older homes. Extreme heat may make an outdoor queue unsafe. A new transit line may change car ownership and curb demand. These are empirical questions. Use census data, administrative records, surveys, public comments, and direct usability testing, while stating the limits of each.

Anecdotes and trends serve different purposes. One resident's ticket can reveal a failure mode. It cannot establish prevalence. A citywide completion rate can estimate scale. It can hide a neighborhood or language group with severe drop-off. Use both without allowing either to do the other's job.

The civic-tech response should be specific and testable. Keep a staffed counter and phone option during rollout. Accept more than one form of address verification. Provide translated instructions and accessible design. Warn residents before enforcement. Publish a privacy statement explaining what data are collected and shared. Review results after sixty and one hundred eighty days. If disparities appear, change the process and test again.

Social change is visible inside this small project. Moving a service online changes routines, staff roles, resident expectations, and the evidence a city can see. It may reduce travel and waiting for many people. It may also make residents with low digital access less visible because they never complete the form. Good administration does not assume innovation is equitable. It defines the intended benefit, watches distribution, and preserves a workable path for people the new system misses.

Three recall questions.

First, how are prejudice, discrimination, and institutional discrimination different?

Second, what three demographic processes directly change a population?

Third, what evidence should a city examine before calling a digital service equitable?

First, prejudice is an attitude, discrimination is an action, and institutional discrimination occurs when established rules or routines produce unequal outcomes.

Second, fertility, mortality, and migration directly change a population.

Third, the city should examine completion and drop-off, time and errors, appeals, alternative-channel use, and differences among affected groups where measurement is lawful, private, and statistically responsible. It should combine those patterns with resident experience and usability testing.

Artifact: Write a one-page equity and urban-change briefing for the parking portal. Include the institutional rule, the groups likely to encounter different barriers, one population or neighborhood trend, one environmental consideration, the evidence you would collect, and one response measurable after sixty days.
