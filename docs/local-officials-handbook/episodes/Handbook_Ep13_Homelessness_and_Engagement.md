## Transcript

Since 2024, the Constitution no longer stops a city from enforcing camping bans when shelter is unavailable. The practical limits now come from state funding rules, property and disability law, and how much shelter a city actually has.

In City of Grants Pass versus Johnson, decided in June 2024 by a six to three vote, the United States Supreme Court held that enforcing general camping laws is not cruel and unusual punishment, even when no shelter is available. That overturned a Ninth Circuit rule that had shaped West Coast policy for five years. But limits remain. Homeless people's belongings are protected from unreasonable seizure. The Americans with Disabilities Act still applies; one city was allowed to clear an encampment but had to give disabled residents more time. And a California law that took effect in 2026 bars cities from penalizing anyone for giving homeless people food, water, blankets, or medical care.

The money comes with conditions. The state's next round of homelessness grants requires a compliant housing element and encampment policies that follow state guidance, which makes housing element compliance a homelessness funding issue too. In Los Angeles County, Measure A, a half-cent sales tax approved in 2024, raises about a billion dollars a year. Torrance's share for 2026 and 2027 from its local solutions fund is about five hundred ninety-seven thousand dollars, and that money cannot pay for police or for prosecuting homeless people. Any enforcement ordinance needs a different source.
In Torrance, the main shelter is a village of forty small cabins that opened in 2022, where the average stay is close to a year. In September 2025, the council directed staff to draft an ordinance allowing arrest after repeated refusals of shelter, paired with low-cost tent shelters. Whether it was adopted should be confirmed with the city clerk. The published counts of how many people are homeless in Torrance conflict, so check the county's own figures before citing one.

The second half of this episode is about the people who don't come to hearings. About forty-five percent of Torrance households rent, and forty percent of residents speak a language other than English at home. Yet the people who testify are mostly older homeowners. Good engagement closes that gap before an item reaches the dais.
Start by saying what level of engagement you are offering. Informing people is different from consulting them, and consulting is different from sharing the decision. Most hearings consult, which means the public is owed a summary afterward of what they said and what changed because of it.
Then go where people already are: school pickup lines, apartment complexes, the transit center, cultural festivals. Partner with groups residents already trust, like congregations and schools. Offer childcare, food, interpretation, and sometimes a stipend. Use randomly selected panels or valid surveys to balance testimony. Petaluma did this in 2022, paying thirty-six randomly chosen residents to spend about ninety hours deciding the future of its fairgrounds. Vallejo let residents as young as sixteen vote on how to spend millions in capital funds. And Torrance Unified's phone policy survey drew more than four thousand responses, far more than any hearing ever will.
For more, see the homelessness and engagement sections of the Local Official's Handbook.
