## Transcript

If you run for office, your votes on a commission become your platform, and your opponents will read the minutes. The best time to start building a record is now, while your reasoning is fresh.

Keep a simple voting log. After each meeting, write down the date, the item, what kind of decision it was, staff's recommendation, your vote, the result, and one sentence on why you voted as you did. For anything involving a permit or contract, note the applicant and their agents. That log helps you vote consistently, gives you material for a campaign, and doubles as your donor screen under the Levine Act.

Explain your decisions the right way for the kind of decision it is. On a quasi-judicial item, explain your vote by the findings: say which findings you could make, which you couldn't, and the evidence that decided it. That protects the record and shows the public that the reason was the law, not preference. On a legislative item, speak to values and policy, and do it plainly.
A few habits build a reputation over time. Be predictable on process, even when you are unpredictable on outcomes; people forgive disagreement more easily than surprises. Read the whole packet, and let it show in a specific question. Write a short public explanation of a decision once a quarter, but only after the item is final, never while a quasi-judicial matter is pending. File your financial disclosure early and step aside visibly when you must. Build relationships across the dais and with staff; in Torrance nothing passes the council without four votes, and learning to count to four starts on a commission. And keep track of your promises. When you say you will ask staff to look into something, follow up in public.

Then keep learning. Three dates are worth planning around: the American Planning Association's California conference each fall, the League of California Cities academy for mayors and council members in January, and its planning commissioners academy in March. The Institute for Local Government offers free planning commissioner training videos. The California School Boards Association runs a five-course Masters in Governance for anyone considering the school board. And for listening, UCLA's Housing Voice podcast explains housing research clearly and often.

Finally, consider building something lasting. No citizens academy turned up for Torrance or the South Bay, though nearby Cypress runs a free six-week program where residents meet the mayor, council, city manager, and department heads and learn how planning, budgets, and public safety work. Proposing one for Torrance would create a pipeline of informed residents and future commissioners, and it would be a fitting first project for someone preparing for a bigger seat.
For more, see the section on building your record, and the training section, in the Local Official's Handbook.
