## Transcript

Two bodies of state law now decide most of what happens at a planning commission: the California Environmental Quality Act, known as CEQA, and a stack of housing laws passed since 2017. Before opening any housing staff report, find out which of these applies. The answer decides how much discretion you actually have.

CEQA requires agencies to study and disclose environmental effects before approving projects. Its key test is the fair argument standard: if substantial evidence supports a fair argument that a project may have a significant effect, a full environmental impact report is required, even if other evidence points the other way. Residents' personal observations can count as that evidence, which is why a packed room can force a full study of a project that isn't exempt.
In 2025 the Legislature passed the biggest change to CEQA in a generation. Housing on infill sites of up to twenty acres, consistent with the general plan and zoning and built at reasonable density, is now fully exempt. A project that misses an exemption by a single condition gets a review limited to that condition. And rezonings that carry out a housing element are exempt too. Expect fights to shift from whether a study was adequate to whether a project really meets each exemption requirement.

Now the housing laws. The housing element and the regional housing needs allocation set the target: Torrance must plan for 4,939 homes between 2021 and 2029, including more than 2,400 for very low and low income households.
The Housing Accountability Act says a project that meets objective standards can be denied or reduced only if the city finds, by a preponderance of the evidence, a specific adverse impact on public health or safety, based on written objective standards, that cannot be mitigated. Saying a building doesn't fit the neighborhood is not a lawful reason. Fines start at ten thousand dollars per unit.
The Housing Crisis Act lets an applicant lock in the rules with a preliminary application, caps hearings on a compliant project at five, and bars cities from reducing zoned capacity. In 2025 it was made permanent.
Other laws take some projects out of the commission's hands entirely: streamlined approval where a city is behind on its targets, lot splits and duplexes on single-family lots, accessory dwelling units, starter homes on small lots, and affordable housing on church land. The state density bonus law lets developers who include affordable units build more, with concessions and waivers the city can deny only on narrow grounds.

For Torrance, the most important issue is the housing element itself. The state found it compliant in 2022 on the condition that the city rezone sites by early 2025, and in March 2025 the state warned that compliance could be revoked if the rezoning ran late. Losing compliance opens the door to the builder's remedy, which lets qualifying projects exceed local zoning.
Torrance met much of its rezoning through a 2023 Housing Corridor Overlay of about ninety-five acres in seven areas, where housing is allowed by right. But in late 2025, a court ruling against neighboring Redondo Beach held that an overlay which allows housing without requiring it, over zoning that permits non-residential uses, cannot count toward the target. Whether that ruling exposes Torrance is one of the most important questions a commissioner can ask the city attorney today.
For more, see the planning commissioner section of the Local Official's Handbook.
