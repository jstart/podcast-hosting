## Transcript

Most of what makes an official effective happens in the details of a meeting: how you read the packet, what you ask, how you make a motion, and how you handle a hard room. Torrance has its own rules of order. It does not use Robert's Rules or Rosenberg's Rules, though most neighboring cities use Rosenberg's, so it helps to know both.

Read a staff report in this order. Start with the recommendation and the findings you are being asked to make. Then check which law governs the item, and whether state housing law limits your discretion. Next, the environmental determination and its evidence. Then the conditions of approval: do they connect to the project's impacts, and can they be enforced? Then the plan policies staff cites, and any it leaves out. Then public letters, especially factual claims staff hasn't answered. Then the alternatives staff did not recommend. And finally, the deadlines, including how many of the five housing hearings have been used.

Ask questions that build the record, not speeches. What evidence supports finding four? is a better question than saying you are worried about traffic. Send detailed technical questions to staff a day or two ahead, so the answers are ready. Ask staff to respond on the record to factual claims from the public. Ask the applicant whether they would accept a condition before you propose it. And ask the city attorney, in public, what your discretion is when a state law may apply.

A good land use motion has three parts: the environmental action, the decision, and the findings and conditions. For example: I move to find the project exempt under the infill exemption, and to approve the use permit based on the findings in the staff report, with finding six modified as discussed, subject to the conditions in the exhibit, with a new condition limiting delivery hours. Under Torrance's council rules, a motion needs a second, only one substitute motion may be pending at a time, a tie fails, and reconsideration may be moved only by a member on the winning side or one who was absent.

If you chair, keep the same sequence every time: the staff report, clarifying questions, the applicant, public comment, rebuttal, close the hearing, deliberation, motion, and vote. Speak last, and make a motion only when no one else will. Restate the motion before the vote, and announce the result, including who voted no.
When a room turns hostile, the law gives you tools and limits. You must first warn a disruptive person that continuing may lead to removal, and may remove them if they don't stop. If a group makes the meeting impossible, the room can be cleared, but the press may stay. You may not, however, prohibit criticism of the body. The craft is mostly prevention: explain the process and the legal limits at the start, apply time limits equally to every side, call a short recess before tempers break, and thank even the angriest speakers.

Deliberate out loud on the findings, not your preferences. Say which findings you can make and which you can't, and point to specific evidence. If the discussion reveals missing information, continue the item with specific direction rather than deciding on a thin record.
For more, see the meeting craft section of the Local Official's Handbook.
