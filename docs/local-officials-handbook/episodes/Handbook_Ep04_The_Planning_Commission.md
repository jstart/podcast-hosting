## Transcript

The planning commissioner's job has changed more in the last ten years than any other local seat. On most housing projects, state law now limits the commission to objective standards, fixed deadlines, and a cap of five hearings. A great commissioner today knows which projects are still discretionary, writes findings that survive in court, and uses the discretion that remains as well as possible.

Everything starts with the general plan, the city's long-range constitution for land use. State law requires elements on land use, circulation, housing, conservation, open space, noise, safety, and, where there are disadvantaged communities, environmental justice. The elements must agree with each other, and zoning must agree with the plan. Torrance adopted its current general plan in April 2010, built around the idea of a balanced city with a deliberate mix of homes, shops, and industry. A focused update now in review aligns that plan with the housing element and adds climate, heat, and evacuation policies.

The commission's core work is entitlements. In Torrance, a conditional use permit requires eleven separate findings, including compatibility with the neighborhood, a suitable site, adequate access and services, and no harmful noise or hazard. Variances work differently here than in many cities: the commission holds the hearing and recommends, and the City Council decides.
Whatever the entitlement, one 1974 California Supreme Court case governs how you decide it. Topanga Association for a Scenic Community versus County of Los Angeles requires findings that bridge the analytic gap between the raw evidence and the decision. In practice that means four habits. Track each required finding word for word. Point to specific evidence in the record for each one: a page of the staff report, a plan sheet, a study, or testimony. Explain why the contrary evidence is less persuasive. And when you deny a project against staff's advice, say why on the record and ask staff to bring back a written denial resolution, rather than improvising findings from the dais.

Conditions of approval have limits too. Under a line of United States Supreme Court cases, a condition must have an essential connection to the project's impacts and be roughly proportional to them. In 2024, the Court applied that same test to impact fees adopted by legislation, not just to fees set case by case.

Then there are appeals. In Torrance, a commission decision can be appealed to the council within fifteen days by the applicant or anyone adversely affected. Every appeal hearing counts toward the state's five-hearing cap on compliant housing. In court, the challenger is limited to the issues raised at the hearing or in letters delivered before it, and must sue within ninety days. That makes the commission's record the lawsuit.
A few habits run a strong hearing. Read the recommended findings and conditions before the plans. Ask staff, on the record, which state law governs a housing item and what discretion remains. Ask staff questions about the code and the evidence, and ask applicants about design and operations. Adopt the environmental action first, then the decision. And when the room is hostile, explain the legal limits at the start, apply time limits equally, and explain your vote by the findings.
For more, see the planning commissioner section of the Local Official's Handbook.
