## Transcript

In Torrance, power on the City Council comes down to one number: four. The city charter requires four yes votes out of seven for any ordinance, any resolution, and any payment of money. A three to two vote, with two members absent, fails. An urgency ordinance needs five. The mayor has no veto and no administrative power, so a mayor's influence comes from setting the tone, framing the debate, and building a majority.

Torrance uses the council-manager form of government. The council sets policy. The city manager, hired by the council and serving at its pleasure, runs the city. The charter forbids the council or any member from directing the manager to hire or fire anyone, or pressuring the manager on contracts or purchases. The city attorney, also appointed by the council, represents the city as a whole, not any single member.
One practical detail matters a great deal: the city manager controls the agenda. The council's rules let the manager submit the agenda and withdraw items, and they set out no formal way for a council member to place an item. In practice, members raise requests at the end of each meeting. A written referral rule would be a worthwhile reform.

The budget is the council's biggest decision each year. In the 2025 to 2026 budget, the general fund brought in about three hundred five million dollars. Property tax and sales tax together made up forty-four percent of it, and public safety took fifty-eight percent of spending. The city's economic reserve is about seventy million dollars, more than a fifth of the general fund, a dramatic recovery from 2020, when unassigned reserves had fallen below one million dollars. A half-cent sales tax, Measure SST, approved in 2022, brings in about twenty-seven million dollars a year.
Pensions are the long-term pressure. In 2020, Torrance borrowed about three hundred fifty million dollars to pay down its pension debt. Even so, the three CalPERS plans for police, fire, and other employees still carry roughly two hundred sixty-six million dollars in unfunded liability. Add a self-insurance shortfall of about fifty-two million dollars and more than two hundred million dollars in facility needs, and the picture is a city that has recovered but has little room for new ongoing costs.

Labor contracts are where much of that money is decided. Under the state's public employee bargaining law, the city must bargain in good faith over wages and working conditions. If talks fail, a union may request factfinding, and afterward the council may impose its last offer after a public hearing. The council gives its negotiators instructions in closed session, but only after naming them in public.

Torrance moved to district elections in 2018, with six districts and a mayor elected citywide. Elections are held in even years on the date of the statewide primary. The current council includes several new members, and the 2026 mayor's race was decided by about two points, so coalitions are fluid. The issues waiting for any new member include the refinery and its hydrofluoric acid, the Metro K Line route chosen against the city's wishes, airport noise, homelessness, housing law compliance, and the long-term budget.
The best habit for a council member is simple. Count to four before every vote.
