## Transcript

Every local official in California wears two hats, and the most important skill in the job is knowing which one you have on. On some items you are a legislator: you make policy for everyone. On others you are something closer to a judge: you apply rules that already exist to one applicant's request. The law treats those two roles very differently, and most of the trouble good officials get into comes from mixing them up.

Legislative work is the general plan, a zoning ordinance, a budget, a fee schedule, a new policy. Here you are free to have opinions, to campaign on them, to meet with anyone, and to vote your values. Courts give legislative decisions wide deference. They ask only whether the decision was arbitrary or entirely lacking in support.
Quasi-judicial work is a use permit, a variance, a design review, an appeal, a permit revocation. Here a specific party has rights. You owe them a fair hearing before a reasonably impartial decision-maker, a decision based on the evidence in the record, and written findings that connect the evidence to the result. Talk about the project privately, prejudge it publicly, or decide it on facts the parties never saw, and a court can throw the decision out.

The four seats this series covers lean different ways. A planning commissioner spends most of the time in the judge's chair, deciding permits and recommending plans. A city council member or mayor is mostly a legislator, but becomes a judge again on every appeal. A school board trustee is mostly a legislator too, setting budgets and policy, but acts as a judge in student expulsions. And an advocate holds no seat at all, but shapes what everyone else decides.
In Torrance, those seats look like this. The City Council has seven members: six elected by district and a mayor elected citywide, each serving four-year terms with a two-term limit. The Planning Commission has seven members appointed by the council. The Torrance Unified School District board has five trustees, each elected from a trustee area. Above all of them sit Los Angeles County, a set of regional agencies, and the state.

Before every agenda item, ask three questions. First, which hat am I wearing: legislative or quasi-judicial? Second, who actually holds the final decision: this body, a higher body on appeal, or the state? Third, what does the record need to contain for this decision to survive a challenge? Those three questions will carry you through most of what follows.
State law in this area changes every legislative session, so treat everything in this series as a starting point, and confirm current rules with your city attorney or district counsel before relying on them.
For more, see the opening section of the Local Official's Handbook.
