## Transcript

Two state laws shape how every local body does its work in public. The Ralph M. Brown Act governs meetings. The California Public Records Act governs documents. Both changed recently, and both catch experienced officials who assume the old rules still apply.

Start with what counts as a meeting. Under the Brown Act, a meeting is any gathering of a majority of a body to hear, discuss, deliberate, or take action on something within its jurisdiction. Notice the word hear. A majority sitting together at a developer's presentation can be a meeting, even if nobody says a word.
The bigger trap is the serial meeting. A majority may not use a series of communications, directly or through intermediaries, to deliberate outside a public meeting. That covers a chain, where one member tells a second, who tells a third. It also covers a hub, where a staff member or a lobbyist polls each member in turn. Staff may brief members one at a time, but they may not tell you where your colleagues stand. If a manager ever says three of your colleagues are fine with this, the briefing has just become a violation.
Social media has its own rule, made permanent in 2026. You may post, answer questions, and ask for input on public platforms. But you may not respond to a colleague's post about a matter in your jurisdiction, and responding includes a like or an emoji.

Agendas must be posted seventy-two hours before a regular meeting and twenty-four hours before a special one. Items not on the agenda can be acted on only in an emergency, when a need arose after posting and two thirds of the body agree, or when an item was continued from a meeting no more than five days earlier. Otherwise you can respond briefly, ask a question, or ask staff to put it on a future agenda.
Closed session is limited to a short list: real property negotiations, litigation, personnel matters, labor negotiations with a negotiator named in public first, and security threats. Final actions and votes are reported out afterward, and leaking what was said inside is itself a violation.

Remote attendance now runs on several tracks. The traditional rule lets a member join from anywhere, as long as that location is listed on the agenda and open to the public. A newer just cause rule lets a member join from an unlisted location for reasons like childcare, illness, or official travel, as long as a quorum is together in person and the remote member is on camera. Those just cause appearances are capped at a few meetings a year.
Beginning July first, 2026, councils in cities the size of Torrance carry new duties: two-way remote public comment, a plan for when the connection fails, a meetings web page, and translated agendas for any language spoken by twenty percent or more of residents who have limited English.

Now the records. The agency has ten days to decide whether records can be released, with one extension of up to fourteen days for listed reasons. And here is the rule officials most often forget: under a 2017 California Supreme Court decision, messages about public business on your personal phone or personal email are public records. The city can ask you to search your own accounts and swear to what you found. Never delete a text thread with an applicant.
For more, see the legal ground rules section of the Local Official's Handbook.
