## Transcript

Many of Torrance's biggest constraints are set by bodies where the city shares a seat, or has none at all. Knowing who they are, and when their decisions are still open, is one of the quickest ways to become more effective than your title suggests.

The most time-sensitive is the Southern California Association of Governments, known as SCAG. It sets each city's housing target and writes the regional transportation plan. Torrance shares a district on SCAG's eighty-six member council with Carson and Lomita. The method for the next housing targets is being written between now and mid 2027, with hearings in spring and summer 2027, a draft allocation in April 2028, and appeals that year. The time to shape Torrance's next number is during the methodology, not during the appeal.
Los Angeles Metro decides rail and bus investment. Torrance has no seat on its board; it is represented by its county supervisor and a city seat held by Inglewood's mayor. In January 2026, Metro chose a K Line route along Hawthorne Boulevard over the city's objection. It is expected to open in late 2036, pending funding.

The South Coast Air Quality Management District regulates the refinery. Its rule on hydrogen fluoride has been suspended since 1992, and in 2019 its board accepted voluntary safety measures instead of a ban. A 2026 federal bill would phase the chemical out within five years. The county's Airport Land Use Commission reviews plans and zoning near Zamperini Field, and the council can overrule it only by a two-thirds vote with findings. The county's boundary commission, known as LAFCO, would decide any annexation of El Camino Village, which a city study estimated would cost twenty-seven million dollars to start and run about eleven million a year in the red.
On the coast, Torrance has no certified local coastal program, so the Coastal Commission itself issues permits at Torrance Beach and the bluffs. Water comes through the Metropolitan Water District, where Torrance has its own director, and through regional groundwater and wholesale agencies.

Transportation law has changed too. Under CEQA, traffic delay is no longer an environmental impact. The measure is vehicle miles traveled. Torrance's thresholds treat a project as significant if its driving per resident or employee exceeds eighty-five percent of the county average, unless it screens out as small, transit-adjacent, or fully affordable. Delay still matters for general plan consistency and conditions, but it cannot make a project significant under CEQA. Separately, a 2022 law bars minimum parking requirements within half a mile of a major transit stop. Parts of Torrance near the transit center may already qualify, and no city map has been published.

To influence bodies you don't sit on, work through the people who do: Torrance's SCAG representative, the South Bay Cities Council of Governments, and the county supervisor's office. Comment in writing while the work is still open, during methodology and rulemaking, not at final adoption.
For more, see the regional bodies and transportation sections of the Local Official's Handbook.
