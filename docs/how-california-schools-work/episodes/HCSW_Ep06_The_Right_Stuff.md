## Transcript

What are students actually supposed to learn? It's a crowded list, and every subject has passionate defenders.

It starts with standards: statements of what students should know and be able to do in each grade. In 2010, California adopted the Common Core State Standards in English and math, which emphasize reasoning and evidence over memorization. The state has its own standards in science, based on the Next Generation Science Standards, and frameworks for history, the arts, health, and more. Standards describe the goal. Curriculum is the specific materials and lessons, and that's mostly chosen by each district from lists the state reviews.
A common worry is whether school is challenging enough. Students can earn good grades while doing work well below grade level. Studies of real classroom assignments often find that many are too easy for the standards they're supposed to meet. There's a concrete way to see this in high school: California's public universities, the UC and CSU systems, require a specific sequence of fifteen courses known as the "a-g" requirements, covering subjects from history to lab science to a world language, and only roughly half of California's high school graduates actually complete that full sequence. Rigor doesn't mean more homework. It means work that asks students to think.

Reading is the foundation of everything else. A child who can't read well by around third grade has a much harder time in every subject afterward. There's been a national shift toward what's called the science of reading: explicit, systematic teaching of phonics, alongside vocabulary and background knowledge. California has moved in this direction with new laws requiring early screening for reading difficulties, including dyslexia, and investments in literacy coaches.
Math is the subject where California students struggle most, and where pandemic losses were steepest. The state adopted a revised math framework in 2023 after a heated debate about how to balance access for all students with acceleration for those ready to move faster. Science, technology, and engineering round out STEM, and access to advanced courses still varies widely from school to school.

Tests are how the system checks whether students are learning. California's main state tests are called CAASPP, taken each spring in grades three through eight and once in high school. English learners also take a separate test, the ELPAC, that measures their progress in English. Tests have real limits and can be overused. But without some common measure, it's hard to know which students need help or which schools are falling behind.
Technology has transformed how schools deliver lessons, especially since the pandemic, when nearly every student got a device. The evidence is clear that technology helps when it's used for specific purposes, like practice with instant feedback, and less clear when screens simply replace teachers. Having a device isn't the whole story, though: many lower-income students still face what's called the homework gap, relying on public WiFi or limited, unreliable internet at home to get schoolwork done, which is why school-provided devices and connectivity matter just as much as what's in the classroom. Artificial intelligence is the newest wave, and schools are still working out rules for how students and teachers should use it.

Then there's everything that makes school feel worth attending. Engagement rises when students see a connection between school and their lives. The arts, music, drama, visual art, and dance, build creativity and give many students a reason to show up. In 2022, California voters passed Proposition 28, which dedicates ongoing funding to arts and music education. Physical education and sports build health, teamwork, and focus, and the state has recently put real weight behind unstructured play too: Senate Bill 291, sometimes called "Recess for All," took effect for the 2024-25 school year and requires at least thirty minutes of daily recess while barring schools from taking recess away as punishment. Field trips expose kids to places and ideas they might never otherwise encounter.
Career technical education, sometimes called CTE, connects learning to jobs in fields like health care, construction, technology, and agriculture. Many high schools now offer career pathways, internships, and dual enrollment, where students take community college classes for credit while still in high school.

Some subjects are about who students become. Social-emotional learning, or SEL, teaches skills like managing emotions, setting goals, and working with others. Service learning connects academic work to helping the community. Character education aims at habits like honesty and persistence. These areas are sometimes controversial, with debates about whether schools or families should lead.
Civics, history, and geography prepare students to understand and participate in democracy, and the stakes are higher than they might seem: surveys have found that only about half of American adults can name all three branches of government, and roughly a quarter can't name any of them at all. California offers a State Seal of Civic Engagement to recognize students who show civic knowledge and action.
World languages matter in a state as multilingual as California. After voters passed Proposition 58 in 2016, schools gained more freedom to offer bilingual and dual-language programs, where students learn in two languages, and that measure effectively reversed an earlier one, Proposition 227 from 1998, which had required English-only instruction in most classrooms. Students who master two languages can earn the State Seal of Biliteracy on their diploma.
And a newer addition: personal finance. California passed a law requiring high school students to complete a semester of personal finance before graduating, being phased in over the coming years. Budgets, credit, and loans, finally in the curriculum.
