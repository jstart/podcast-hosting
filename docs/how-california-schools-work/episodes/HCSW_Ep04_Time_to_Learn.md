## Transcript

Here's a simple truth that's easy to forget: learning takes time. Today we look at how much time California students get, and how well it's used.

Let's start early. The years before age five are when the brain develops fastest, and children arrive at kindergarten with huge differences in vocabulary and early skills. Good early education can narrow those gaps. The key word is good. Studies of high-quality programs show lasting benefits, while mediocre programs show gains that fade.
California has made a big bet here. The state has been phasing in transitional kindergarten, often called TK, a free year of school for four-year-olds before regular kindergarten. As of the 2025–26 school year, TK became available to all four-year-olds in California public schools. The challenge now is quality: enough trained teachers, small enough classes, and classrooms designed for young children, not just shrunken first grades.

Next, class size. Smaller classes sound obviously better, and in the early grades there's good evidence that they help, especially for disadvantaged students. But class size reduction is expensive, because every smaller class needs another teacher and another room. In the late 1990s, California funded a big push to shrink K–3 classes. It needed so many new teachers so fast that many under-prepared teachers were hired, and the benefits were smaller than hoped. The lesson: class size matters, but it's not the only way to give students more individual attention, and it can crowd out other investments.

How long is the school day? California sets minimum instructional minutes by grade, and the state's school year is typically around 180 days. It turns out that's not short by international standards at all. Compared with most other countries, American students actually spend more time in the classroom, not less, according to data compiled by the OECD. So the real challenge isn't a shortage of scheduled time. It's what happens to that time once the doors are open. A lot of it disappears into transitions, announcements, assemblies, test prep, and classroom disruptions. Researchers who've followed a class through a day often find the real amount of focused learning time is much smaller than the schedule suggests. Protecting that time is one of the cheapest improvements a school can make. California has also used the schedule itself as a tool for helping students: a 2019 law, SB 328, requires California high schools to start no earlier than 8:30 in the morning and middle schools no earlier than 8:00, reflecting research that teenagers in particular do better in school when they get a bit more sleep.

When students fall behind, the most effective single fix in the research is intensive tutoring. Not homework help once a week, but frequent sessions, several times a week, in small groups of one to four, during the school day, with a consistent tutor using good materials. Some programs have shown gains equivalent to months of extra learning. After the pandemic, many districts invested in tutoring, with mixed results depending on how closely they followed that recipe.
Summer is the other big lever, though it comes with a caveat worth naming. The idea of the "summer slide," where students lose ground academically over a long break, gets repeated often, but researchers actually disagree about how real or how large it is. Some studies do find a genuine loss, particularly in math. Others argue that much of what looks like decline is really just a temporary dip that fades once the school year gets going again. What's less disputed is that kids without access to camps, books, and enrichment simply have fewer chances to keep learning and growing over the summer than kids who do. Summer programs can help close that gap, but only if students actually attend and the program has real learning in it.
After-school programs fill a similar role during the school year. California funds a large network of them through a program called ELO-P, the Expanded Learning Opportunities Program, which aims to give students in many districts access to a full nine-hour day of school and enrichment, plus summer weeks. Good programs mix homework help, arts, sports, and a safe place to be.

Finally, the most basic time issue of all: showing up. A student who misses ten percent or more of school days, about eighteen days a year, is considered chronically absent. Chronic absence rose sharply after the pandemic and is still well above earlier levels in California. Missing school early is linked to weaker reading by third grade, and missing school later is linked to dropping out. There's also a direct financial reason this matters for schools, not just for students: California funds districts based largely on average daily attendance, not simply how many students are enrolled, so when kids aren't in their seats, it's the school's budget that takes a hit too. The causes of absence are often solvable: illness, transportation, family crises, or a student who feels no one would notice they're gone. Schools that track attendance closely and reach out personally, especially with a friendly call rather than a threat, tend to bring kids back.
