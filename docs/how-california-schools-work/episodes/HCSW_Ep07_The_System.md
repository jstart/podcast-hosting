## Transcript

So far we've looked at the pieces: students, teachers, time, places, and what's taught. Now we zoom out to the machinery that connects them. Fair warning: this is the chapter with the most acronyms.

Start at the top. Under the California Constitution, education is a state responsibility. Local school districts exist because the state created them. The state sets graduation requirements, adopts standards, funds most of the budget, and writes the laws districts must follow. Several players share power in Sacramento. The Legislature passes education laws and the budget. The Governor proposes the budget and can veto bills. The State Board of Education, appointed by the Governor, sets policy on standards, tests, and accountability. And the State Superintendent of Public Instruction, elected by voters, runs the California Department of Education. When those players disagree, things can get complicated.
The federal government plays a smaller role than many people think. Federal dollars make up well under a tenth of school funding in a typical year, closer to one-twentieth. That share briefly climbed higher during the COVID relief funding of 2020 through 2022, but in normal years it has been sliding for decades. But those dollars come with strings. Federal law requires annual testing in certain grades, public reporting of results by student group, extra support for low-income students through what's known as Title I, and services for students with disabilities. Federal civil rights law also protects students from discrimination. Small money, big influence.

Most of the day-to-day decisions happen in school districts. California has about a thousand of them, ranging from Los Angeles Unified, with several hundred thousand students, to tiny rural districts with a single school. Each is governed by an elected school board, usually five or seven members, who hire a superintendent, approve the budget, negotiate with unions, and set local policy. School board elections often have low turnout, which means a small number of voters have a lot of say. Charter schools add another layer: there are well over a thousand of them in California, and each one counts as its own kind of local education agency, operating somewhat outside the traditional district structure even though most are authorized by a district.
Between the state and the districts sit fifty-eight county offices of education. They review and approve district budgets, which gives them an early-warning role when a district is heading toward financial trouble. They also run programs that small districts can't manage alone, like certain special education services and schools for students in the juvenile justice system.

Who else shapes policy? Teachers' unions are among the most influential. The two big statewide unions are the California Teachers Association and the California Federation of Teachers. The legal backdrop matters here: the Rodda Act of 1975 established collective bargaining rights for California's teachers, and the U.S. Supreme Court's 2018 decision in Janus v. AFSCME ended mandatory "fair share" union fees for public employees, which changed how unions fund themselves and organize members. Locally, unions bargain contracts covering pay, class size, hours, and working conditions. At the state level, they're major players in elections and lawmaking. Supporters see them as the voice of educators and a check on bad management. Critics say they sometimes block changes that would help students. Either way, very little happens in California education without them.
California also lets voters make policy directly through ballot initiatives. Some of the most important education rules in the state, especially around money, came from voters rather than legislators. We'll see a couple of big ones in the next chapter. And there are other influencers: parent groups like the PTA, school administrator and school board associations, advocacy organizations, researchers, philanthropists such as the Hewlett Foundation, which has funded education policy work in California for years, and business groups.

How does anyone know if schools are doing their job? That's accountability. California's current approach is built around the California School Dashboard, which reports on several measures instead of a single score. We'll dig into it in Episode 9. Schools and districts where student groups perform poorly across multiple measures are identified for extra help. Under state law, that help mostly comes from county offices and a state agency called the California Collaborative for Educational Excellence. Under federal law, the lowest-performing schools must develop improvement plans, and federal law specifically targets intervention at the bottom five percent of schools statewide by performance. There's no single fix that works everywhere; what turns one struggling school around often doesn't work at the school down the road. California leans toward support rather than harsh penalties.
Finally, a document every parent should know about: the LCAP, or Local Control and Accountability Plan. Every district, county office, and charter school must write one and update it each year. It explains the goals, the actions to reach them, and how the money will be spent, especially money meant for high-need students. By law, districts must involve parents, students, teachers, and the community in developing it, and must hold public hearings before the board adopts it. If you want to influence your district's priorities, the LCAP process is one of the most direct ways in.
