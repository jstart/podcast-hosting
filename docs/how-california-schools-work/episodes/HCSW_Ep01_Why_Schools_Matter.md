## Transcript

Over ten episodes, we're going to take apart one of the biggest, most important, and most confusing systems in the state: public education. California enrolls somewhere around six million students in its public schools, taught by something like three hundred twenty thousand teachers, spread across roughly eleven thousand public schools. That's more kids than many states have people. If you're a parent, a teacher, a voter, or just someone who cares, this series is meant to give you the map.

Let's start with a blunt question. How are California's schools doing?
The honest answer is: it depends on what you compare them to. On the national test known as NAEP, sometimes called "the Nation's Report Card," California has for years landed near the middle of the pack, often a bit below it. But California also serves an unusually high share of students who are learning English and students from low-income families. When researchers adjust for those differences, the picture shifts, and some groups of California students do as well as or better than similar students elsewhere.
Zoom out further and you can compare the whole United States to other wealthy countries. On international tests, American students actually score above the average of other wealthy countries in reading and science, and close to the average in math — not clearly below it, as people sometimes assume. What stands out isn't that the U.S. is failing across the board. It's that the gap between our highest and lowest performers is unusually wide.

Why does any of this matter? Because the economy has changed. A century ago, you could build a solid life on physical work and a basic education. Today, the jobs that pay well increasingly reward the ability to read closely, reason with numbers, solve unfamiliar problems, and learn new tools quickly. Artificial intelligence is speeding that up. When software can do routine tasks, the human skills that remain valuable are judgment, communication, and adaptability. Those are exactly the skills schools are supposed to build.
And when schools fall short, the costs don't stay inside the classroom. Adults without a high school diploma earn less on average, pay less in taxes, are more likely to rely on public assistance, and are more likely to end up in the criminal justice system. Economists have tried to put a dollar figure on this, and the estimates run into hundreds of thousands of dollars per student over a lifetime. Education isn't only a private good for the student. It's a shared investment for everyone.

Here's a tricky part. Most parents think their own child's school is doing fine, even when they believe schools in general are struggling. Psychologists have a name for this kind of optimism, and it shows up everywhere, not just in education. It's reinforced by grades. Grades have crept upward over the decades, so a report card full of A's and B's doesn't always mean a student is on track for college-level work. One of the best habits a parent can build is to look at more than one signal: grades, yes, but also state test results, teacher conversations, and the actual work the child brings home.
So are schools getting better? Over the long run, yes. Graduation rates have climbed. More students take advanced courses. Scores for younger students rose meaningfully between the 1990s and the 2010s. Then the pandemic hit, and learning dropped sharply, especially in math and especially for students who were already behind. Recovery has been real but uneven, and in many places it's still incomplete.

To understand where we are, it helps to know where we came from. American public schools grew out of a nineteenth-century idea, championed by reformers like Horace Mann, that a democracy needs educated citizens and that the public should pay for it. The system expanded to include high schools, then kindergarten, then, after long struggles, students it had excluded: Black students, students with disabilities, students who don't yet speak English. Some of those struggles have names you'd recognize. In 1954, the Supreme Court's decision in Brown v. Board of Education outlawed school segregation. In 1972, a law called Title IX opened the door to equal opportunity for girls and women in schools, in the classroom and beyond it. In 1975, what we now call IDEA guaranteed students with disabilities a public education built around their needs. And in 1982, the Supreme Court ruled that undocumented children have the same right to attend public school as any other child. Each of these expansions came with new laws, new court decisions, and new debates that in some cases are still going on today.
Which brings us to the deepest question: what are schools actually for? People give different answers. Some say preparing kids for jobs. Some say preparing them to be citizens. Others say helping each child discover what they're capable of, or passing on a shared culture, or giving every kid a fair shot regardless of where they started. When researchers have actually put that question to people directly, the answers split roughly three ways in close to equal measure: about a third point to academic preparation, about a third to preparing kids for jobs and careers, and about a third to preparing citizens who can take part in a democracy. Schools try to do all of these at once, and a lot of the arguments you hear about education are really arguments about which purpose should come first.

Over the next nine episodes, we'll follow a simple sentence. Education is students, teachers, time, places, and the right stuff to learn, organized into a system, with resources, aimed at success. Then we'll ask: so now what?
