## Transcript

Ask almost anyone about the best part of their education and they'll name a teacher. Research backs that up. Inside a school, nothing matters more to student learning than the quality of teaching. So today: who teaches in California, how they get there, and what keeps them.

California has roughly three hundred thousand public school teachers. Most enter the profession because they want to make a difference for kids. But teaching competes with every other career for talented people, and when the job looks stressful, isolating, or underpaid compared to alternatives, fewer people sign up. California has gone through repeated shortages, especially in special education, math, science, and bilingual education, and especially in high-poverty and rural schools.
To teach in a California public school, you generally need a credential issued by the Commission on Teacher Credentialing. The usual path is a bachelor's degree, a teacher preparation program that includes supervised student teaching, and passing assessments of subject knowledge and teaching skill. The state has been experimenting with other routes: residencies where new teachers train alongside a mentor for a full year while earning a stipend, internships that let people teach while finishing coursework, and programs that help classroom aides become teachers. During shortages, districts also fill gaps with emergency permits, which means some students are taught by people who haven't finished training.

Getting teachers in the door is only half the problem. Keeping them is the other half. You'll sometimes hear it said that half of new teachers quit within five years — that's actually a myth. The real numbers are more encouraging: somewhere between seventy and eighty-three percent of new teachers are still teaching five years later. Most stay. Still, a meaningful minority do leave early, for familiar reasons: heavy workloads, challenging classrooms without enough support, weak leadership, and pay that makes it hard to afford housing in much of California. The first few years are the hardest, and good mentoring during that stretch makes a real difference.
There's also the question of where teachers end up. Experienced teachers often have more choice about their assignments, and many choose schools with fewer challenges. The result is that students in high-poverty schools are more likely to have new or not-yet-fully-credentialed teachers. It isn't anyone's plan. It's what happens when the system doesn't actively push the other way.

Teachers improve with practice, feedback, and time to learn from each other. Traditional professional development, a one-day workshop on a topic someone else chose, has a weak track record. What tends to work better is coaching tied to the actual curriculum, and regular time for teachers to plan lessons together, look at student work, and figure out what's working. In many countries with strong school systems, teachers spend much more of their week on that kind of collaboration and less in front of students than American teachers do.

Now, the money. California teacher salaries are among the highest in the nation in raw dollars. But California is also one of the most expensive places to live, so the advantage shrinks a lot once you account for housing. Pay is usually set by a salary schedule negotiated between each district and its teachers' union. Teachers move up the schedule with years of experience and additional education units, not with performance — which is a big reason so many teachers go on to earn a master's degree. That's worth a caveat, though: research generally finds a master's degree doesn't do much to improve teaching effectiveness, except in math and science.
Benefits are a big part of compensation. Health insurance costs have risen steadily, and every dollar spent on benefits is a dollar not available for raises or classroom needs. Pensions are the largest long-term benefit. Most California teachers belong to CalSTRS, the state teachers' retirement system, and for a long time many didn't get full Social Security benefits either, because of two federal rules, the Windfall Elimination Provision and the Government Pension Offset, that reduced benefits for people with a pension from work not covered by Social Security. In January 2025, the Social Security Fairness Act repealed both of those rules, delivering an estimated seventeen billion dollars to more than three million affected retirees nationally. A teacher who stays a full career can retire with a solid pension. A teacher who leaves after a few years gets far less. Funding those pensions has become a major pressure on district budgets over the past decade.

How do teachers know if they're doing well? Formal evaluations happen, but in many districts they're infrequent and rarely lead to specific improvement. California's teacher-evaluation law, known as the Stull Act, leaves most of the specifics to districts, and the results show it: almost every teacher is rated satisfactory. A well-known 2009 study, sometimes called "The Widget Effect," found that evaluation systems like this tend to rate nearly everyone satisfactory regardless of how they're actually performing. There's an ongoing debate about whether student test scores should play a role. California has largely stayed away from that approach.
Then there's tenure, officially called permanent status. In California, teachers typically earn it after two years. After that, dismissal requires a documented process. And when districts must lay off teachers, seniority generally decides who goes first: newest out. Supporters say these protections guard against unfair firing and favoritism. Critics say they make it hard to remove weak teachers and that seniority-based layoffs hit high-poverty schools hardest, since that's where newer teachers cluster. Both points have evidence behind them.
