## Transcript

If you want to understand any system, follow the money. California's school funding is famously complicated, but the core ideas fit in one episode. Let's go.

First, how much? California's K–12 system spends well over a hundred billion dollars a year from all sources. Per student, that works out to roughly twenty thousand dollars or more in recent years, counting state, local, and federal money. That sounds like a lot, and compared to decades past, it is. But compared to other states, California has spent less per student than many for most of the last forty years. It has climbed toward the national average recently. And California's high costs mean each dollar buys less. Salaries, housing, construction, and land all cost more here. You can see that in the classroom, too: California's student-to-teacher ratio is one of the highest in the country, well above the national average, which means each dollar of spending is stretched across more kids per teacher than in most states. So when people argue about whether California spends enough, part of the disagreement is whether you adjust for what money can buy.
What does it buy? Mostly people. Roughly eighty to eighty-five percent of a typical district's budget goes to salaries and benefits for teachers, aides, counselors, administrators, custodians, bus drivers, and other staff. That's why decisions about pay, pensions, and staffing ratios dominate school budgets.

Where does the money come from? About two-thirds comes from the state, a smaller share from local property taxes, and roughly a tenth from the federal government, with some variation year to year. There's a fourth, much smaller source too: the California Lottery, a name everyone recognizes even though it barely moves the needle. It contributes around one percent of school budgets, a few hundred dollars per student, but it adds up — more than thirty-four billion dollars for schools since 1985. The state-local-federal split wasn't always the case, though. It changed because of two ballot measures you should know by name.
The first is Proposition 13, passed in 1978. It capped property taxes at one percent of a property's value and limited how fast assessed values can rise until a property is sold. Before Prop 13, local property taxes were the main source of school money, and wealthy districts could spend far more than poor ones. That inequity had already reached the courts: in 1971, the California Supreme Court ruled in Serrano v. Priest that funding schools mainly through local property taxes was unconstitutional, because it let wealthy districts spend so much more per student than poor ones. Prop 13 didn't fix that inequity directly, but it did change who controlled the money. After Prop 13, local revenue dropped, and the state took over most school funding. Control over school money shifted from local communities to Sacramento.
The second is Proposition 98, passed in 1988. It set a minimum funding guarantee for schools and community colleges, calculated through formulas tied to state revenue, enrollment, and the economy. In practice, it usually means a large share of the state's general fund, often around forty percent, goes to education. Prop 98 protects school funding, but it also means school budgets rise and fall with the state's tax revenue, which in California depends heavily on income taxes paid by high earners and can swing a lot.

Once the state has its education money, how does it hand it out? Since 2013, mostly through the Local Control Funding Formula, or LCFF. It replaced dozens of separate programs with a simpler idea. Every district gets a base grant per student, adjusted by grade level. Then it gets extra money, called supplemental grants, for each student who is low-income, an English learner, or in foster care. Districts where those students make up more than fifty-five percent of enrollment get an additional concentration grant. The idea is that students with greater needs should bring more resources, and that local districts, not Sacramento, should decide how to use it. That's also why the LCAP from last episode exists: it's how districts show what they're doing with that extra money.
Not everything flows through LCFF. Some money is still earmarked for specific purposes, often called categorical funds. The biggest is special education, which gets its own funding stream. Others support things like school meals, after-school programs, and the arts money from Prop 28.

Is money well spent? It's a fair question, and the answer is: sometimes. Research now shows fairly clearly that money matters, especially for low-income students, when it's spent on things with good evidence behind them, like strong teachers, early education, and tutoring. It matters less when it goes to things that don't touch learning. Most districts aren't wasting large sums, but many could get more out of what they have. That's a separate question from outright misuse, though — districts undergo mandatory financial audits every year, and county offices of education provide additional oversight, so there's a formal check against money being flatly misused, apart from the harder question of whether every dollar is spent as effectively as it could be.
If Californians want more for schools, there are only a few ways to get it. The state can raise taxes, which requires a two-thirds vote of the Legislature or voter approval. Voters have extended higher income tax rates on top earners through ballot measures. Locally, districts can ask voters to approve bonds for facilities and parcel taxes for operations. A parcel tax is a flat tax on each property. Most parcel taxes still need a two-thirds vote when a school board places the measure on the ballot, which is a high bar. But a 2021 ruling by the California Supreme Court opened another path: when a parcel tax reaches the ballot through voter initiative, meaning a citizen petition rather than a school board vote, it only needs a simple majority to pass. Communities that pass them can fund things the state doesn't cover.
And there's one more resource that doesn't show up on the budget: volunteers. Parents, grandparents, and community members who tutor, coach, chaperone, and raise funds through PTAs and foundations add real value. It's a kind of stealth wealth, and it's unevenly distributed, which is worth keeping in mind.
