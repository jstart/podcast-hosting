## Transcript

Over nine chapters, we've walked through the whole system: students, teachers, time, places, what's taught, the machinery, the money, and the scoreboard. Now the question that matters most: what do we do with all this?

Some people look at a system this complex and say it should be blown up and rebuilt. Replace districts with networks of independent schools. Give every family a funding account to spend wherever they choose. Let technology replace much of classroom teaching, through personalized online platforms like Khan Academy, or through all-online charter schools built around the idea that kids can move at their own pace. These ideas have real appeal, and pieces of them have been tried. The evidence suggests disruption can work in specific places when it's carefully managed, and can do real harm when it's rushed. Schools are not startups. Kids only get one third grade.
Others argue the fix is simple: more money. It's worth asking what well-funded schools actually do with their resources, and it helps to put a number on it. California public schools spend somewhere in the range of eighteen to nineteen thousand dollars per student each year. Many elite private schools spend fifty thousand dollars or more. Wealthy districts and private schools tend to buy smaller classes, more counselors, richer arts and sports programs, better-paid and more experienced teachers, and lots of individual attention for students who fall behind. Those are good things, and research suggests money spent this way helps, especially for students who start with less. It's also fair to note that private schools can choose which students they admit, so some of their strong results come from who attends, not just from what the extra money buys.
Still others argue the opposite: that tight budgets force schools to focus and innovate. There are schools that do remarkable things with modest resources. But there's little evidence that scarcity itself makes schools better. More often, lean budgets mean cutting the very supports that help struggling students. The honest middle ground is that money matters, and how it's spent matters just as much.

So how does change actually happen in education? Slowly, and usually through a combination of forces. Research builds a case. Advocates and parents push for it. Lawmakers or voters change the rules or the funding. And then, the hardest part, thousands of educators have to actually change what they do every day.
There's a simple test that can help you tell whether any of this will actually matter. It's sometimes called the Ed100 Razor: does the change put different people in the room, or does it change how kids and teachers actually spend their time? If a reform doesn't do one of those two things, it probably won't change much, no matter how good it sounds on paper. Big reforms often fail not because the idea was wrong, but because the implementation was rushed, underfunded, or ignored the people who had to carry it out. Lasting change tends to come from steady, focused improvement over years, not dramatic announcements.

Which brings us to you. You don't need to be an expert or an official to make a difference. Here are a few places to start.
Learn how your own school and district are doing. Look them up on the California School Dashboard, and look at the results for each student group, not just the average.
Show up. Attend a school site council, a PTA meeting, or a school board meeting. Most boards have a public comment period, and a thoughtful, specific comment from a parent carries weight.
Get involved in the LCAP. Every district must gather community input on its plan each year. Ask how the extra money for high-need students is being used, and whether it's working.
Vote in school board elections, and look at local bond and parcel tax measures carefully. These are decided by small numbers of voters, which means your vote goes further.
And support the adults in the building. Teachers, aides, counselors, and principals are doing hard work. Volunteering, thanking them, and backing good ideas makes a real difference.

If you want to keep learning, there are excellent resources. EdSource covers California education news. CalMatters, a nonprofit newsroom, covers California policy, including education. The Public Policy Institute of California and Policy Analysis for California Education publish accessible research. The California Department of Education's website has data on every school. The Legislative Analyst's Office, the state's nonpartisan budget and policy analysis office, digs into how education dollars are spent and what they buy. And the free Ed100 course at ed100.org, which inspired the structure of this series, goes deeper on every topic we've covered, lesson by lesson, in English and Spanish.

Every student deserves a great education, and a great education system doesn't happen by accident. It happens because people understand how it works and decide to help. Now go make a difference.
