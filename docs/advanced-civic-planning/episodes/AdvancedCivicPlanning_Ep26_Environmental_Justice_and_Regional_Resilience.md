## Transcript

Imagine a Torrance workshop on extreme heat, flooding, and industrial land. The regional map shows several hazards, but the room hears different stories. A renter describes an apartment that holds heat overnight. A worker waits for a bus beside heavy traffic with no shade. A business owner worries about flood access. A planner can map hazards, but environmental justice asks who is exposed, who is sensitive, who has resources to adapt, and who helps choose the response.

This episode is preparation, not legal advice about a general plan, environmental document, or equity finding. Environmental-justice duties, climate projections, funding rules, and regional tools change. Use current statutes, adopted plans, authoritative data, community evidence, and advice from the Torrance city attorney.

Environmental justice joins environmental conditions with fairness in public decisions. It examines the distribution of pollution, hazards, benefits, and public investment. It also examines procedure: who receives notice, who can participate, whose knowledge counts, and whether participation changes the decision.

California general-plan law requires qualifying jurisdictions to address environmental justice in the general plan, either in a separate element or through integrated goals and policies. Required subjects include pollution exposure and other issues specified by current law, along with objectives and policies that reduce health risks, promote civic engagement, and prioritize improvements and programs for disadvantaged communities. The exact duty must be read from current statute and local circumstances.

Do not treat environmental justice as a label placed on one neighborhood. Conditions vary block by block and across populations. A community can face high pollution exposure without fitting one screening-tool threshold. A regional percentile is a comparison, not a complete description of a person's life.

Use a four-part baseline. First, map burdens: air pollution, traffic exposure, contaminated sites, noise, heat, flood, wildfire smoke, water stress, or other locally relevant hazards. Second, map people and sensitivity: age, disability, health, housing condition, outdoor work, language, income, vehicle access, and other factors supported by appropriate data. Third, map adaptive capacity: shade, cooling, insurance, savings, transit, health care, social networks, emergency communication, and public facilities. Fourth, map public investment and decision history.

The fourth layer matters because resilience is not only physical. A neighborhood that repeatedly receives plans without funded projects may distrust another engagement process. A community with strong local organizations may recover faster even when income measures are low. Qualitative evidence can explain what a numeric index misses.

SCAG's April 2023 public Toolbox Tuesday on integrating equity and adaptation into planning emphasized bringing those lenses into the planning and environmental process rather than adding them after project selection. Its 2024 Environmental Justice Tools for Local Planning session presented resources for local analysis. Its 2024 heat-proofing training addressed resilience planning and mitigation. These materials support a workflow, not a single mandatory score.

Regional resilience asks how connected systems behave under stress. Electricity powers cooling and communication. Transportation provides evacuation, work access, and medical trips. Water systems depend on energy and infrastructure. Housing condition affects indoor heat and smoke. A failure in one system can cascade into another city.

SCAG's 2025 water-resilience training and 2026 Regional Resilience Toolkit session add this regional perspective. Hazards cross boundaries, watersheds, utility territories, and transportation networks. Torrance cannot plan port access, air quality, water reliability, and evacuation as if city limits were walls.

Resilience has at least four stages. Prepare by reducing known vulnerability. Withstand by keeping critical functions operating during an event. Recover by restoring services and housing without displacing people. Adapt by changing investments and standards as conditions and projections change.

Test every strategy across all four. A cooling center helps during heat but fails if residents cannot reach it, do not know it exists, or fear leaving a pet. Tree planting can reduce heat but requires water, maintenance, species selection, and time. Home retrofits may help owners while renters remain exposed. Flood protection can shift water or raise nearby property costs. A good strategy anticipates secondary effects.

Use future climate conditions where the decision requires them. Historic averages alone can understate heat, precipitation, sea-level, or fire-smoke risk over the life of infrastructure. State and regional tools provide scenarios, not certainties. Record the scenario, time horizon, resolution, and uncertainty.

For practical application, conduct an equity-and-resilience stress test for a fictional Torrance corridor plan. Choose one hazard, such as extreme heat. Identify exposure, sensitive populations, and adaptive capacity. Trace two systems, such as transit and electricity. Describe what happens during a three-day event, including nighttime conditions.

Then evaluate three investments. One can be site-scale, such as shade and cool materials. One can be neighborhood-scale, such as a resilient community facility. One can be regional, such as coordinated transit or grid planning. For each, identify beneficiaries, burdens, responsible agency, funding source, maintenance, performance measure, and potential displacement effect.

Engagement should be designed as infrastructure. Budget for translation, interpretation, childcare, accessible formats, trusted messengers, and compensation where lawful. Offer more than one time and method. Return to participants with a decision log showing what changed, what did not, and why.

Avoid extracting stories to decorate a predetermined plan. Ask residents what conditions they observe, what coping strategies they use, and which interventions would create new burdens. Combine that knowledge with health, environmental, and infrastructure data. Protect privacy when the population or geography is small.

Prioritization needs explicit criteria. Possible criteria include severity, number of people exposed, vulnerability, community priority, readiness, co-benefits, cost, maintenance capacity, and whether the investment corrects a historic gap. Publish the weights. Test whether a different reasonable weighting changes the result.

Regional coordination does not erase local accountability. Name the action Torrance controls, the action requiring a neighboring jurisdiction or agency, and the forum where coordination will occur. Add a trigger for revisiting the plan after a new climate projection, major event, or infrastructure failure.

Measure both distribution and outcomes. Count where money goes, but also track indoor temperature, shade coverage, service continuity, travel time to relief, exposure reduction, housing stability, and participant assessment. Disaggregate carefully enough to reveal differences without exposing individuals.

Use a scenario register to keep assumptions visible. For each hazard, record the projection source, planning horizon, severity case, affected assets, exposed populations, and confidence level. Add an early-action threshold, such as repeated service interruption or a revised heat projection. Then name the person or agency responsible for reviewing the trigger. This turns adaptation from a distant aspiration into a managed cycle. It also prevents a commission from treating one forecast as permanent truth. When evidence changes, update the scenario, explain the change publicly, and test whether the investment sequence still protects the people facing the greatest combined burden.

Details change. Screening tools and resilience toolkits inform judgment, but they do not replace current legal requirements, local knowledge, or city-attorney advice.

Now three recall questions.

Question one. What four layers belong in an environmental-justice baseline?

Answer. Burdens, people and sensitivity, adaptive capacity, and the history of public investment and decisions.

Question two. What are the four stages of resilience used in this lesson?

Answer. Prepare, withstand, recover, and adapt.

Question three. Why should a city analyze regional systems?

Answer. Hazards and infrastructure networks cross city boundaries, and failure in energy, water, transportation, housing, or communication can cascade across jurisdictions.

Your learner artifact is the equity-and-resilience stress test. Include one hazard profile, two connected systems, three investments at different scales, an engagement plan, and a public decision log template. Cite public data and label uncertainty.
