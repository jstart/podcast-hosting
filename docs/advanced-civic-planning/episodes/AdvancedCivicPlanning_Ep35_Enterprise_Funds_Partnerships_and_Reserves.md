## Transcript

Imagine Torrance reviewing three ways to modernize a public service. The city could operate it through an enterprise fund supported mainly by service charges. It could contract or partner with another organization. It could use reserves to manage transition and risk. These are not three interchangeable sources of easy money. Each is a governance choice about who pays, who controls, who bears risk, and how service continues.

This lesson is educational preparation, not financial, legal, rate, accounting, procurement, labor, tax, investment, or municipal advisory advice. Enterprise operations, partnerships, and reserve use depend on current law, contracts, cost studies, labor obligations, city policy, and specific facts. Use qualified staff and advisers for real decisions.

An enterprise fund accounts for a service financed and operated in a manner similar to a business activity, often with user charges as a primary support. Common municipal examples can include water, sewer, sanitation, transit, airport, or other services, depending on the city.

Businesslike accounting does not mean the service has only a profit goal. The public mission may include reliability, safety, environmental compliance, affordability, and universal access. Rates and charges must be evaluated under applicable legal standards and city policy.

Build a cost-of-service picture. Include operations, labor, administration, billing, maintenance, capital renewal, debt service, reserves, regulatory compliance, customer assistance, and shared services. Allocate shared costs consistently and transparently. Underpricing can defer failure. Overcollection or misallocation can create legal and equity concerns.

Evaluate rates using revenue sufficiency, stability, fairness, affordability, conservation signals, administration, and legal defensibility. A low current rate can be fiscally expensive if it produces abrupt future increases or asset deterioration. Use a multi-year plan.

Partnership is a broad word. It may describe a service contract, lease, concession, joint powers arrangement, development agreement, nonprofit collaboration, or public-private partnership. The label says little about actual risk.

Start by defining outputs. What service, quality, access, response, maintenance, data, reporting, and handback condition must be delivered? Then allocate each risk to the party best able to manage it at reasonable cost.

Create a risk table for design, construction, schedule, demand, revenue, operating cost, labor, regulation, technology, cybersecurity, insurance, force majeure, asset condition, and termination. A contract does not make risk disappear. If the private party cannot perform, the city may still face public pressure to preserve an essential service.

Test value for money against a realistic public alternative. Include transaction, oversight, financing, contract-change, and termination costs. Private financing can shift timing, but the cost of capital and required return may be higher. Public delivery may offer control but expose the city directly to delivery risk. Compare whole-life outcomes.

Protect flexibility. Long terms can support investment while reducing the city's ability to respond to technology, demand, law, or community priorities. Include performance reviews, audit rights, data ownership, change procedures, remedies, step-in rights where appropriate, and exit plans.

Transparency matters before and after award. Define procurement method, evaluation criteria, conflicts, assumptions, contract summary, performance reporting, and amendment approval. A complex partnership should not become less visible after signing.

Reserves are resources set aside for uncertainty, timing, emergencies, stabilization, asset replacement, or other defined purposes. They are not idle money merely because they are unspent today. A reserve policy should state purpose, target, permitted uses, replenishment, authority, reporting, and relation to other funds.

Different risks call for different reserves. Revenue stabilization addresses volatility. Emergency reserves address shocks. Working capital addresses timing. Asset replacement reserves prepare for renewal. Debt reserves may be required by financing documents. Do not collapse them into one unexplained balance.

Measure reserve adequacy with risk, not a borrowed rule of thumb alone. Consider revenue volatility, expenditure flexibility, disaster exposure, infrastructure condition, cash-flow timing, insurance, intergovernmental dependence, debt obligations, and access to liquidity. Policy ranges can guide judgment, but the rationale matters.

Using reserves for a one-time transition can be sound. Using them to support a recurring structural gap without a correction plan merely delays the decision. Every reserve draw should show the remaining balance, risk exposure, and replenishment path.

Consider a hypothetical Torrance organics-processing service. The city is comparing expanded in-house operations, a regional partnership, and a long-term private contract. The example is invented.

For the enterprise model, estimate rates, volumes, operating cost, renewal, compliance, billing, customer assistance, and reserve targets.

For the regional option, examine governance, voting, cost allocation, capacity rights, performance, withdrawal, and liability.

For the private contract, examine pricing, escalation, volume guarantees, contamination rules, asset ownership, labor transition, data, insurance, default, step-in, and handback.

For all three, test lower volume, higher regulation cost, facility outage, and contractor failure. Ask how quickly the city can restore service and who pays.

Suppose reserves could lower the first-year rate impact. Show whether that is transition smoothing or an ongoing subsidy. If the new recurring cost remains, spending reserves does not solve the structural gap. A staged rate plan plus targeted assistance may be more transparent, subject to legal review.

Use separate decision gates. First, confirm service requirements. Second, compare delivery models. Third, select financing and rate strategy. Fourth, approve contracts and reserve use. This prevents financing excitement from deciding the service model prematurely.

Add a reconciliation rule. Each year, compare forecast volume, cost, revenue, asset condition, partner performance, and reserve balance with actual results. Explain variances and update the multi-year plan. If a contract amendment shifts risk back to the city, show its full fiscal effect before approval.

Watch transfers between funds. A shared-service payment may be legitimate, but it should follow a documented allocation method. A transfer can also obscure whether an enterprise is self-supporting or receiving general support. Present gross operations, transfers, and net position separately.

Finally, plan for failure while relationships are healthy. Identify records, equipment access, licenses, staff knowledge, customer data, and temporary operating authority needed if a partner exits. Test the continuity plan periodically. An exit clause is useful only if the city can act on it.

Your learner artifact is a Service Sustainability Map. Include:

Public service outcomes.

Enterprise cost-of-service categories.

Five-year revenue and rate drivers.

Delivery alternatives.

Risk-allocation table.

Value-for-money comparison.

Contract oversight and exit plan.

Reserve purposes and targets.

Proposed draws and replenishment.

Affordability and distribution.

Stress scenario and continuity plan.

Decision gates and owners.

End with the sentence: the service remains sustainable if, followed by your three most important conditions.

Now, three recall questions.

Question one: Does an enterprise fund remove the public mission?

Answer: No. Businesslike accounting supports a public service that may also require reliability, affordability, access, safety, and compliance.

Question two: What is the central rule of partnership risk allocation?

Answer: Assign each risk to the party best able to manage it at reasonable cost, while recognizing the city may retain ultimate service responsibility.

Question three: When does a reserve draw hide a structural problem?

Answer: When one-time resources repeatedly support recurring costs without a credible correction and replenishment plan.
