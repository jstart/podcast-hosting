## Transcript

Imagine a Torrance agenda item proposing a neighborhood resilience and mobility center on city-owned land. The concept combines a shaded transit stop, emergency supply space, community rooms, bicycle facilities, a small commercial kiosk, and stormwater improvements. A grant may fund part of construction. A partner may operate programming. Supporters see many benefits. Skeptics see cost and complexity.

Your capstone task is not to advocate immediately. It is to build a decision record that lets the public and officials see purpose, alternatives, full cost, revenue, risk, equity, and accountability.

This episode is educational preparation, not financial, legal, accounting, engineering, environmental, planning, real estate, tax, investment, or municipal advisory advice. The capstone is hypothetical. For an actual Torrance project, use current official documents and qualified city staff, counsel, auditors, engineers, planners, appraisers, and financial professionals.

Step one is frame the decision. Write a need statement without naming the project. For example: provide reliable neighborhood space for daily mobility, community use, and emergency support while improving heat and stormwater resilience. Then list required outcomes and constraints.

Identify decision authority and process. What body acts? What planning, environmental, procurement, property, funding, rate, or contracting approvals may be required? Mark legal questions for counsel. Do not treat the capstone as a substitute for required analysis.

Step two is establish evidence. Create a source register. For Torrance facts, look to current operating budgets, Capital Improvement Plans, budget reviews, ACFRs, public agenda reports, project dashboards, adopted plans, fee schedules, contracts, and other official records. Add county or state sources when relevant. Record title, date, page, link, and what the source supports.

Distinguish fact, estimate, assumption, and policy choice. Facts are documented conditions. Estimates apply methods to evidence. Assumptions fill uncertainty transparently. Policy choices express priorities. Do not let one category masquerade as another.

Step three is define alternatives. Include the proposed combined center, a smaller phased version, separate improvements at existing sites, and a baseline. Screen each for authority, feasibility, timing, and ability to meet minimum outcomes.

Step four is map life-cycle cost. Include land opportunity cost, design, engagement, environmental work, construction, equipment, grants administration, staffing, utilities, cleaning, security, programming, information technology, insurance, maintenance, renewal, contract oversight, and eventual decommissioning. Assign owner, timing, range, and source to each.

Create an asset register. Include shelter, pavement, drainage, landscaping, lighting, cameras if proposed, bicycle equipment, restroom systems if proposed, furnishings, kiosk, and technology. Give each a maintenance owner and renewal year.

Step five is map resources. List grant proceeds, General Fund support, restricted funds, service charges, rent, partner contributions, financing, and developer-related sources only if legally and factually relevant. Label each one-time or ongoing, restricted or flexible, confirmed or uncertain, and gross or net.

Do not count the grant until conditions, match, reimbursement timing, eligible cost, and deadline are understood. Do not count rent without vacancy and management cost. Do not call borrowing revenue. Do not assume a restricted balance can support unrelated operations.

Step six is assess fiscal impact. Build a baseline and incremental model. If the city-owned site currently has a use or value, include it. Separate construction-period activity from stabilized annual performance. Show effects by fund and by year.

For any property-tax claim, use the city's applicable share and authoritative allocation information, not the full tax bill. For retail revenue, test whether spending is new or shifted from another Torrance business. For service cost, ask departments about marginal workload and capacity thresholds.

Step seven is evaluate public value. Score service quality, safety, access, equity, climate resilience, plan consistency, neighborhood effect, economic opportunity, deliverability, and fiscal sustainability. Publish definitions and weights. Add confidence ratings and sensitivity.

Step eight is test delivery. Compare direct city operation, a nonprofit or public partner, and a private contract where feasible. Define outputs and allocate design, construction, demand, operating, regulatory, technology, and continuity risks. Include oversight, data, audit, remedies, termination, and handback.

Step nine is test financing. Compare pay-as-you-go, phasing, grants, and any lawful borrowing alternative. If debt is considered, match term to asset life, show total repayment, identify security, model stress, and disclose long-term administration. Financing should follow the service and project strategy.

Step ten is set reserves. Identify construction contingency, operating stabilization, emergency, and asset replacement needs. Avoid using one reserve label for every uncertainty. Show permitted use and replenishment.

Now run scenarios. The expected case uses documented central assumptions. The downside case delays the grant reimbursement and kiosk opening while raising operating cost. The stress case combines construction escalation, lower revenue, and partner failure. For each, identify actions, owners, and decision points.

Conduct an equity review. Who benefits daily? Who benefits in emergencies? Who travels farther? Who pays charges? Who bears construction or displacement effects? What accessibility and language needs exist? Could targeted assistance preserve access without obscuring full cost?

Conduct a pre-mortem. Imagine the center is struggling five years after opening. Possible causes include ambiguous ownership, weak programming demand, high cleaning cost, failed equipment, vacancy, grant noncompliance, or an inflexible contract. Convert each plausible cause into mitigation or an approval condition.

Then prepare a recommendation. Options include proceed, proceed with conditions, phase, redesign, seek more evidence, or reject. A conditional approval should name conditions, deadlines, owners, and what happens if they are not met.

Create a monitoring dashboard before approval. Include schedule, capital budget, grant status, operating forecast, reserve level, asset readiness, partner performance, usage, service quality, incidents, revenue, maintenance work orders, and equity measures. Set reporting frequency and thresholds for council review.

Your learner artifact is the Capstone Fiscal Evaluation Packet.

Section one: one-page executive decision brief.

Section two: need, outcomes, authority, process, and source register.

Section three: baseline and at least three alternatives.

Section four: life-cycle cost, asset register, and ten-year operating forecast.

Section five: revenue authority and reliability map.

Section six: incremental fiscal impact by fund and phase.

Section seven: public-value matrix, equity review, and sensitivity.

Section eight: delivery, partnership, financing, debt, and reserve analysis.

Section nine: expected, downside, and stress scenarios.

Section ten: recommendation, approval conditions, monitoring, and review dates.

Use no invented Torrance figures. If official information is unavailable, write not established, provide a hypothetical range clearly labeled as illustrative, or identify the responsible office and question.

Now, three recall questions.

Question one: What four labels prevent confusion in the evidence base?

Answer: Fact, estimate, assumption, and policy choice.

Question two: Why must the capstone include a baseline and multiple alternatives?

Answer: Because fiscal and public value are incremental, and a fair decision requires comparison with what happens without the proposal and with other ways to meet the need.

Question three: What makes a conditional approval meaningful?

Answer: Specific conditions, owners, deadlines, evidence requirements, monitoring thresholds, and consequences if conditions are not met.

The capstone habit is simple to remember. Define the public need. Compare real alternatives. Count the whole life. Match resources to obligations. Expose uncertainty. Protect equity and service. Record the reasons. Monitor the promise.

You now have a repeatable fiscal lens for city decisions. Use it with humility, current evidence, professional advice, and an open public process.
