## Transcript

You are buying coffee in Torrance when a neighbor recognizes you from a city meeting. A development item is on next week's agenda. The neighbor begins describing late-night noise near the site and asks how you plan to vote. Later, the applicant sends an email offering a private project tour. Neither contact is automatically proof of misconduct. Both require disciplined handling.

This is study preparation, not legal advice or an ethics opinion. Conflict rules are technical, fact-specific, and subject to change. Do not use this lesson to decide whether any official must participate or recuse. For a real Torrance matter, promptly consult the current staff report, city policies, required disclosure forms, and the city attorney. Advice should be obtained before participating, not reconstructed after the vote.

Ethics begins before criminal conduct. A public official holds delegated power and must use it for public purposes through fair procedures. Several legal systems may apply at once: the Political Reform Act, Government Code section 1090 and related rules concerning public contracts, incompatible-office and common-law doctrines, gift and honoraria limits, campaign rules, local codes, and due-process requirements. Each has different definitions, thresholds, remedies, and enforcement structures. Never collapse them into a single intuition about whether something "looks bad."

Start with financial interests. California's Political Reform Act generally requires a public official to avoid making, participating in making, or using an official position to influence a governmental decision when the official knows or has reason to know of a qualifying financial interest and the decision will have a reasonably foreseeable material financial effect under applicable rules. The analysis can involve sources of income, investments, real property, gifts, and personal financial effects. Exceptions and detailed regulations matter.

The correct habit is early identification. Ask whether you, your spouse or dependent children, or relevant business interests have a connection to a party, property, consultant, or outcome. Do not independently decide that an interest is too small, too far away, or shared by everyone. Give counsel complete facts and follow the required public disclosure and recusal procedure.

Section 1090 is different. It broadly addresses financial interests in contracts made by public agencies and can carry severe consequences. Participation can be attributed differently depending on the official's role, and some interests are defined as remote or noninterests under statute. A planning entitlement is not automatically a contract, but development agreements, reimbursement arrangements, acquisitions, and other transactions may raise contract questions. Get advice before negotiations or preliminary steps, because contract formation can begin before final approval.

Gifts create another track. California regulates gifts to public officials through disclosure, annual limits, and potential disqualification rules. The Fair Political Practices Commission updates dollar thresholds and guidance. Meals, travel, event admission, discounts, and tickets may be gifts depending on facts and exceptions. Never rely on an old dollar amount from memory. Use current FPPC guidance and the agency's policy.

Ethical judgment extends beyond technical disqualification. Using confidential information for private advantage, seeking special treatment for friends, directing staff for personal purposes, or accepting favors that undermine independence can violate law or public trust. At the same time, officials should not recuse merely to avoid a difficult vote when no lawful basis exists. Participation is also a duty. Counsel can distinguish mandatory disqualification, permitted abstention, and ordinary political discomfort.

Now consider ex parte contacts. An ex parte contact is communication about a pending adjudicative matter outside the noticed hearing with less than all interested participants present. Local land-use practice does not treat every such contact identically, and rules may vary by body and decision type. The key risks are undisclosed evidence, unequal access, prejudgment, and inability to respond.

A safe protocol has five steps. First, do not promise a vote or invite a private merits presentation. Second, explain that the matter must be decided under applicable standards on the public record. Third, direct written material to the official city channel so it can be distributed. Fourth, make a contemporaneous note of who contacted you, when, and what substance was conveyed. Fifth, disclose the contact on the record as directed by city policy and counsel.

Site visits deserve the same care. A visit can clarify geography, but it can also expose a commissioner to operations, conversations, or conditions not reflected in the packet. Do not trespass. Avoid coordinated private advocacy. Note the date, route, observations, and conversations. Disclose material information. If a view depends on access the public or other parties did not have, ask staff how to place comparable information in the record.

Predetermination is more than having opinions. Elected and appointed officials are often selected because they have views. The due-process concern in an adjudicative matter is whether a decision maker is capable of considering the evidence and law with an open mind, free from impermissible bias. Statements such as "I will never approve housing on this street, whatever the law says" create a different problem from a policy preference that remains open to the record.

Recusal must be complete. When disqualification is required, the official generally should publicly identify the nature of the conflict to the degree required, leave the decision-making area as applicable, and avoid discussing, influencing, or voting on the matter. There are limited statutory nuances, including circumstances involving interests in real property and participation necessary for a quorum. Those rules demand counsel, not improvisation.

Apply this to Torrance. At the coffee shop, say: "I cannot discuss how I will vote. Please send your observations to the city so they can become part of the record." Record the contact. Before the item begins, disclose that the resident reported late-night noise and that you offered no response on the merits. Ask whether the record should be supplemented or whether anyone needs an opportunity to respond.

For the applicant's tour, route the invitation through staff. Ask whether a staff-led visit open or equally available under city practice would be appropriate. If you attend any authorized visit, avoid negotiation and disclose material observations. A tour is not a substitute for evidence at the hearing.

Suppose you own rental property several blocks from the project. Do not estimate distance from memory and announce that there is no conflict. Give the address, ownership facts, project boundaries, and any likely financial effects to the city attorney. The applicable Political Reform Act regulations and current FPPC guidance determine the analysis. If counsel advises disqualification, follow the full procedure.

Suppose your close friend is the project architect, but you have no financial relationship. That may not fit the same statutory financial-interest rule, yet it can raise bias, appearance, or local-policy concerns. Disclose the facts to counsel. The right result depends on law and context, not on whether you personally believe you can be fair.

Here are three recall questions.

Question one. Why should an official not combine all ethics questions into one appearance test?

Answer. Because financial conflicts, contract interests, gifts, bias, confidentiality, and local ethics rules have different legal elements and remedies.

Question two. What are the five steps for an unexpected ex parte contact?

Answer. Make no commitment, point to the public standards and record, route material through official channels, document the contact, and disclose it as counsel and policy require.

Question three. Is recusal always the safest choice?

Answer. No. Disqualification may be mandatory in some cases, but unnecessary abstention can abandon a public duty. Obtain fact-specific legal advice.

Your learner artifact is a personal ethics decision tree. Begin with financial interests, contracts, gifts, relationships, prior statements, outside communications, and site visits. For each branch, list the facts to collect, the official to consult, the disclosure step, and the required conduct if disqualified. Keep a blank ex parte contact log with date, person, matter, substance, response, and disclosure.
