## Transcript

Imagine three applications arriving in Torrance on the same morning. One adds an accessory dwelling unit behind a house. One proposes apartments on a commercial corridor. One proposes affordable housing on land owned by a religious institution. A single intake label saying housing project is not enough. Each proposal may invoke a different state pathway, with different eligibility tests, standards, labor rules, environmental treatment, and clocks. The first professional task is classification.

This episode is preparation, not legal advice about a project or property. State housing laws change quickly, and many pathways contain detailed exclusions, definitions, transition rules, and local-option provisions. Confirm current statute, current HCD guidance, local ordinances, facts in the application, and advice from the Torrance city attorney.

Ministerial approval means the agency applies fixed standards without discretionary judgment. A ministerial project is generally not subject to ordinary project-level CEQA review because the approval itself is ministerial. That does not mean no rules apply. Planning standards, building and fire codes, subdivision requirements, affordability covenants, labor provisions, tribal consultation procedures, demolition protections, and other requirements may still matter, depending on the law.

Build the decision tree from questions, not bill numbers. Bill numbers remain in conversation long after statutes are amended. Start with the physical proposal. Is it an accessory unit, a duplex, a subdivision, a multifamily development, a mixed-use project, or institutional-land housing? Then identify location, existing uses, affordability, unit count, demolition, environmental constraints, coastal status if relevant, historic resources, hazardous sites, labor commitments, and the jurisdiction's housing status.

The first branch is local ministerial authority. Some cities permit specified projects by right under local zoning even without a special state streamlining statute. If local law supplies a straightforward route, compare it with any state route the applicant invokes. Do not assume a state pathway is the only one.

The second branch covers accessory dwelling units and junior accessory dwelling units. State law requires ministerial treatment for qualifying proposals and limits local standards. The exact category matters because rules differ for converted space, detached units, multifamily properties, setbacks, height, parking, and owner occupancy. Use HCD's current ADU handbook and the current local ordinance. Never rely on a summary from several amendments ago.

The third branch covers small-lot and small-scale housing laws. Senate Bill 9 created a ministerial route for qualifying two-unit development and urban lot splits in eligible single-family residential areas. Later amendments refined eligibility and implementation. A proposal can fail because of site characteristics, tenant protections, demolition history, or another statutory exclusion. Local objective standards may apply, but they cannot physically preclude the units the statute protects.

The fourth branch is the Streamlined Ministerial Approval Process commonly associated with Senate Bill 35 and expanded by Senate Bill 423. It applies to qualifying multifamily developments when statutory conditions are met. HCD uses APR permit data and housing-element compliance status to publish determinations about which jurisdictions are subject to streamlining and whether the applicable affordability threshold is generally 10 percent or 50 percent. The project must satisfy location, affordability, objective-standard, labor, and other requirements.

For this branch, verify the current HCD statutory determination. Do not infer eligibility from a city's reputation or from one year of production. Check the determination date and category. Then test the project itself.

The fifth branch is Assembly Bill 2011, which established ministerial pathways for qualifying housing on certain commercially zoned sites. It contains separate structures for fully affordable development and for mixed-income housing along eligible commercial corridors. Corridor width, parcel frontage, prior use, affordability, density, labor standards, and exclusions can all matter. A map that colors every commercial parcel the same is not a legal eligibility analysis.

The sixth branch is Senate Bill 4. It provides a by-right pathway for qualifying affordable housing on land owned by independent institutions of higher education or religious institutions, subject to statutory conditions. Ownership timing, affordability, location, site exclusions, replacement obligations, objective standards, and labor provisions require verification.

Other routes may belong on the local tree. State density bonus law is not itself one universal ministerial pathway, but it can change density, concessions, waivers, parking, and processing for a qualifying project. Housing-element rezoning programs may require by-right approval for specified projects. Supportive housing, low-barrier navigation centers, farmworker housing, manufactured housing, and emergency shelters have specialized rules. Recent CEQA exemptions under Assembly Bill 130 and Senate Bill 131 may remove or narrow environmental review for qualifying housing even when the underlying entitlement analysis has additional steps. Exemption and ministerial approval are related concepts, but they are not interchangeable.

Use a two-stage gate for every branch. Stage one asks whether the pathway is legally available. Stage two asks whether the submitted project satisfies its conditions. Keep uncertain facts visible. A site may appear eligible until a hazardous-site record, historic designation, prior tenancy, fire-severity classification, or parcel history changes the answer.

Then create a pathway comparison sheet. Columns should include legal route, approval type, affordability requirement, site criteria, excluded conditions, objective standards, demolition and replacement rules, labor requirements, CEQA treatment, review clock, appeal process, and recorded obligations. Add a final column called evidence in file. That column prevents assumptions from becoming findings.

For practical application, classify the three fictional Torrance proposals from the opening. Do not decide them. For the accessory unit, identify which current ADU category might apply and list missing measurements. For the commercial-corridor apartments, compare local zoning, AB 2011, and SMAP without assuming any route wins. For the religious-institution site, test the opening conditions of SB 4 and flag ownership, affordability, and site facts requiring proof.

The commission's role depends on the result. A fully ministerial project may be approved administratively and never come to a discretionary hearing. That is not evasion. It is the process the Legislature or local ordinance selected. Commissioners still shape future outcomes when they recommend objective standards, zoning amendments, specific plans, and housing-element programs.

If a ministerial project appears on an informational agenda, keep comments within the legal frame. Ask which pathway applies, which objective standards were tested, whether deadlines were met, and where compliance documentation can be found. Do not invite a popularity vote that the city has no authority to conduct.

Build the city decision tree as a maintained product. Put the current revision date on it. Link each branch to the statute and official guidance. Assign an owner to update it after legislation. Archive prior versions because an earlier application may be governed by an earlier rule. Include a prominent instruction to involve counsel when pathways overlap.

Avoid the false choice between speed and rigor. A good tree speeds review because it front-loads the right facts. It also improves rigor because every conclusion links to evidence and a current requirement.

Details change. HCD fact sheets are excellent navigation tools, but the operative statute, effective date, local law, and city-attorney analysis control.

Now three recall questions.

Question one. What is the first question in the ministerial decision tree?

Answer. Identify what the project physically proposes and its basic site and affordability facts before selecting a statute.

Question two. Does ministerial approval mean building, fire, affordability, or labor rules disappear?

Answer. No. It means approval applies fixed standards without discretionary judgment. Other applicable requirements remain.

Question three. Why should exemption and ministerial approval appear in separate columns?

Answer. A CEQA exemption addresses environmental review. A ministerial pathway addresses the nature of the approval. A project may require separate analysis of each.

Your learner artifact is a one-page decision tree plus the pathway comparison sheet. Apply both to the three fictional proposals. Mark every unsupported fact as unknown. Add a version date, official source links, and a box titled city-attorney confirmation before final classification.
