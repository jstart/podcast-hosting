## Transcript

A Torrance resident approaches the lectern and says, "The city should simply ban this use everywhere." Another speaker says Sacramento has no right to tell Torrance what to do. Both statements express a view about power. Neither answers the legal question: Where does a California city get authority, and what limits that authority?

This episode is study preparation, not legal advice. Municipal authority changes through constitutions, statutes, regulations, voter enactments, and court decisions. A rule that is valid for one city or subject may be invalid for another. For a real Torrance decision, check the current staff report, charter, municipal code, state and federal law, and city attorney advice as of the decision date.

A city is not sovereign in the same way as a state. It is a public corporation created under state law. California cities are generally either general-law cities or charter cities. Torrance is a charter city. A charter can provide greater control over municipal affairs, but the phrase "charter city" does not mean exemption from state law.

California's Constitution gives charter cities authority over municipal affairs. The state can still control matters of statewide concern when the governing legal test is satisfied. Courts often examine whether there is a genuine conflict, whether the subject is a municipal affair, whether the state law addresses a statewide concern, and whether the law is appropriately tailored under applicable precedent. The analysis is contextual. A subject can have both local and statewide dimensions.

General-law cities derive powers from the state Constitution and statutes. Charter and general-law cities both exercise police power under the California Constitution to make and enforce local regulations not in conflict with general laws. Land-use regulation traditionally occupies an important local field, but state legislation increasingly sets substantive and procedural boundaries, especially for housing.

Preemption is the doctrine that higher law displaces local law. Express preemption occurs when a statute explicitly limits local regulation. Implied preemption can arise when the Legislature has fully occupied a field or when local law duplicates, contradicts, or enters an area that state law reserves under judicial tests. Federal law can also preempt state and local action.

Conflict can be direct. A city cannot require what superior law forbids or forbid what superior law requires. Conflict can also be structural. A local process might frustrate a statutory deadline, hearing limit, or objective approval scheme even if no sentence openly contradicts the statute. That is why adding one more local condition or hearing can carry legal significance.

The hierarchy is more complex than a simple stack. Federal and state constitutions constrain all levels. Federal statutes and regulations operate within federal authority. State statutes, regulations, and judicial decisions define city powers. A city charter allocates local governmental structure and municipal powers within constitutional limits. Ordinances create local law. Resolutions often adopt policies or take specific actions. Administrative rules and permit conditions must rest on delegated authority. Contracts cannot manufacture police power the city does not possess.

Rights limit planning power. The First Amendment can affect signs, assembly, religious exercise, and expressive land uses. Equal protection and due process prohibit certain classifications and arbitrary deprivations. The federal Fair Housing Act and California fair-housing laws constrain discriminatory practices and effects. Disability law can require reasonable accommodation. Takings law limits uncompensated appropriation or regulation under constitutional standards. Conditions demanding property or money can trigger heightened scrutiny concerning connection and proportionality.

These doctrines are not slogans that decide every case. Saying "property rights" does not end zoning analysis, and saying "police power" does not erase constitutional limits. The advanced habit is issue spotting followed by counsel. Identify which governmental act burdens which protected interest, what standard applies, and what evidence supports the city's action.

State housing law illustrates legal layering. California has enacted rules governing housing elements, regional housing needs, density bonuses, accessory dwelling units, replacement housing, permit timing, objective standards, certain streamlined approvals, and limits on reducing residential capacity. Applicability depends on project and jurisdiction facts. A local general plan or zoning provision does not defeat a controlling statute merely because the local rule was adopted first.

At the same time, state law often leaves local choices. Cities may select among compliant sites, adopt objective design standards within legal limits, plan infrastructure, shape public-realm investments, and use legislative processes to define policy where discretion remains. The practical task is to locate the remaining decision space rather than argue abstractly about local control.

Delegation matters inside the city. Voters adopt the charter and may act through initiative or referendum under applicable law. The City Council exercises legislative authority and other powers assigned by law. Commissions act only within authority granted by charter, ordinance, resolution, or statute. Staff administers adopted rules and delegated approvals. A planning commission cannot acquire jurisdiction because a crowd wants it to decide an issue.

Remedies also shape authority. A court may invalidate an ordinance, order an agency to set aside a decision, compel a legally required act, award certain damages or fees, or interpret a charter or statute. State agencies can review or enforce particular programs. Electoral remedies can change officeholders or laws. Administrative appeals may correct errors before litigation. Knowing the remedy clarifies why procedural compliance and a complete record matter.

Apply this to Torrance. Imagine a proposal that qualifies for a state-created housing approval pathway. Residents ask the commission to impose a subjective neighborhood-character test. First ask whether the body has a discretionary decision at all. Then identify which standards must be objective, which local requirements remain enforceable, and whether exceptions or specific health and safety findings are legally available. Do not stage a discretionary hearing when state law assigns a ministerial or streamlined role.

Now imagine Torrance wants to regulate a new commercial use citywide. Ask a legal sequence. Does the police power cover the subject? Does federal or state law expressly or impliedly preempt local regulation? Is the subject a municipal affair, a statewide concern, or both under charter-city doctrine? Does the proposed rule burden constitutional or statutory rights? Is the classification supported by evidence and rational policy? Has the city followed required environmental, notice, and adoption procedures?

Fiscal power is also bounded. Cities rely on taxes, fees, assessments, fines, grants, and intergovernmental transfers under distinct rules. California voter-approved constitutional provisions constrain many revenue measures. A regulatory fee must relate to permissible costs under applicable law. A development impact fee requires statutory findings and relationships. A special tax follows different procedures from a general tax. Planning bodies should not promise funding mechanisms they have not verified.

Intergovernmental immunity and property ownership can complicate local control. State, federal, school, utility, airport, or other public entities may be subject to different land-use rules. A neighboring jurisdiction's project may affect Torrance without being subject to Torrance zoning. Influence may come through consultation, environmental review comments, agreements, regional bodies, or political negotiation rather than direct permitting.

Here are three recall questions.

Question one. What does Torrance's charter-city status mean?

Answer. Torrance has constitutional authority over municipal affairs through its charter, but remains subject to federal law, constitutional limits, and valid state laws addressing statewide concerns under applicable doctrine.

Question two. What is preemption?

Answer. It is displacement of local law by superior law, expressly or by implication under legal tests such as conflict or field occupation.

Question three. What is the best response when state law narrows local control?

Answer. Identify the remaining lawful decision space, apply controlling requirements, and use legislative and administrative tools where local discretion still exists.

Your learner artifact is a legal-authority ladder. For one hypothetical Torrance action, list federal, state, charter, municipal-code, policy, and administrative sources. Beside each, write the power granted, the limit imposed, the responsible decision maker, and the likely remedy for violation. End with a box labeled "unresolved questions for counsel."
