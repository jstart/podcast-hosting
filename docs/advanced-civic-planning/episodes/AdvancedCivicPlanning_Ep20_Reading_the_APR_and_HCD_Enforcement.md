## Transcript

Picture a Torrance commission meeting with a thick spreadsheet attached to the agenda. The title says Annual Progress Report. One column shows housing-element programs. Other columns show applications, approvals, permits, affordability, and units. A commissioner sees a large number of proposed homes and announces that the city has met its housing target. That conclusion may be wrong. The APR uses several milestones, and HCD's central RHNA benchmark is issued building permits. Reading the report well means knowing what each number can and cannot prove.

This lesson is preparation for public service, not legal advice about an APR, enforcement matter, or development proposal. Housing statutes, forms, reporting instructions, and HCD interpretations change. Always use the current reporting year instructions, the current statute, the public record, and advice from the Torrance city attorney.

California Government Code requires cities and counties to report annually on implementation of the general plan, including the housing element. The housing APR covers the prior calendar year and is due to HCD and the Governor's Office of Land Use and Climate Innovation by April 1. HCD publishes forms, definitions, and tools. It also makes submitted data available for public use.

An APR serves several audiences. Local officials use it to check program delivery. HCD uses it to monitor compliance and progress. Residents and advocates use it to compare adopted commitments with outcomes. Researchers use the data to examine broader patterns. One spreadsheet therefore carries legal, managerial, and public-accountability functions.

Begin by separating the pipeline. An application is not an approval. An approval is not a building permit. A building permit is not a completed home. A certificate of occupancy is not necessarily recorded in the same period as the permit. Each milestone answers a different question.

Proposed units show market interest and the possible future pipeline. Approved units show that land-use permission has advanced. Permitted units are the principal measure HCD uses to track progress toward RHNA by income category. Completed units show delivery. Affordable units require attention to the applicable income category and the mechanism that preserves affordability. Do not add unlike milestones and call the sum production.

Read the report in three passes. The first pass is arithmetic. Compare permits with the jurisdiction's RHNA by very low, low, moderate, and above-moderate income categories. Look at annual numbers and cumulative progress across the planning period. Ask whether one category is lagging while another advances.

The second pass is geography and project type. Which neighborhoods and corridors are receiving applications? How much production is accessory dwelling units, multifamily housing, mixed use, or redevelopment? Are inventory sites moving? Are nonvacant sites developing at assumed densities? Geographic concentration may reveal infrastructure readiness, market preference, displacement risk, or an ineffective program elsewhere.

The third pass is implementation. Read the program-status entries against the adopted housing element. Words such as ongoing or in progress are not self-proving. What action occurred during the reporting year? Was an ordinance adopted? Were funds committed? Did outreach reach the intended population? Did the city remove the identified constraint? Does the reported action match the program deadline?

Check definitions before drawing trends. A revised form may add fields or change instructions. A unit may be assigned to an income category through deed restriction, affordability assumptions allowed by current guidance, or another documented method. Accessory dwelling unit affordability methods have changed over time. A comparison across years may require normalization.

Also check for corrections. APR data can be amended. A project name may change. Duplicate records, phased permits, or withdrawn projects can confuse a quick total. Use the submitted report, HCD's current data, and local permit records together when the distinction matters.

The APR is not merely descriptive. HCD says it proactively monitors housing-element programs by evaluating APR data and checking in with jurisdictions. It monitors required rezonings and statutory deadlines. It also investigates stakeholder inquiries and complaints submitted through the Housing Accountability Unit portal.

HCD may learn of a concern from a housing element, an APR, a comment letter, a phone call, an email, a news article, or another source. Its published enforcement process generally begins with fact gathering and communication. Depending on the issue, HCD may monitor, issue a letter of inquiry, provide technical assistance, or request corrective action. A local government is generally given time to respond, often thirty days, although HCD says timing can vary by case.

If the issue is not corrected, HCD can take stronger action within its authority. That can include warning that housing-element compliance may be revoked, issuing a notice of decertification, or notifying the California Attorney General. Other housing laws carry their own remedies, exposure, and court procedures. Do not treat every HCD letter as the same legal event. Read the heading, cited authority, requested response, deadline, and stated next step.

Enforcement is not limited to failure to adopt an element. HCD states that it may review an action or failure to act that is inconsistent with an adopted housing element or housing-element law. Failure to implement a program can matter. So can a rezoning that does not deliver the promised capacity.

For a commissioner, the practical response to an HCD letter is disciplined. Ask staff for the full letter and attachments. Identify the exact program or statute at issue. Separate HCD's factual assumptions from its legal conclusion. Ask what evidence the city will provide, who is preparing the response, and whether a public action is required. Let the city attorney advise on legal strategy. Do not improvise a defense from the dais.

Create an APR dashboard with six boxes. Box one is RHNA progress by income category, using permits. Box two is the broader pipeline, with applications, approvals, permits, and completions kept separate. Box three is program deadlines due this year. Box four is sites-inventory activity and remaining capacity. Box five is fair-housing and displacement indicators that the element says the city will monitor. Box six is correspondence, including HCD questions, responses, and corrective dates.

For each box, add a source field. A number without a source is not ready for deliberation. Add a date because the pipeline changes. Add a short note explaining any local methodology. If an affordability category is estimated, say so. If the report was corrected, preserve the correction date.

Use the dashboard to ask better questions. Instead of asking why housing is low, ask which income category is below the expected pace and which adopted program was intended to address it. Instead of asking whether the city is compliant, ask whether any program deadline is late, whether HCD has raised a concern, and what evidence supports the city's status. Precision makes the conversation fairer to staff, applicants, and residents.

Remember that high permit counts do not excuse a missed mandatory program. Low counts do not alone prove unlawful conduct. The APR is evidence that must be connected to the governing requirement.

Now three recall questions.

Question one. What APR milestone is HCD's key benchmark for tracking RHNA progress?

Answer. Issued building permits, organized by the relevant income categories.

Question two. When is the housing APR generally due, and what period does it cover?

Answer. It is due by April 1 to HCD and LCI and reports on the previous calendar year.

Question three. What are three possible steps in HCD's published enforcement process?

Answer. Examples include fact gathering, a letter of inquiry, technical assistance, a request for corrective action, a warning of decertification, decertification, or referral to the Attorney General. The path depends on the case.

Your learner artifact is the six-box APR dashboard. Populate it with the latest publicly available Torrance APR. Include a data date, source link, and one verification note in every box. End with three agenda questions: one about production, one about program implementation, and one about remaining capacity.
