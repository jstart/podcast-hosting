## Transcript

Picture a Torrance budget workshop. Residents request faster street repairs, expanded library hours, and more emergency preparation. Each request is understandable. Someone then asks why the city cannot simply move money from a healthy-looking account or create a new local tax. The answer begins with a core fact: public revenue comes with legal boundaries, economic behavior, and political consent.

California cities do not operate one unrestricted wallet. They manage many funds and revenue streams, each with its own authority and purpose.

This episode provides educational preparation, not financial, legal, accounting, tax, investment, or municipal advisory advice. Revenue authority and voter-approval requirements depend on current law and specific facts. For a real proposal, consult current statutes, constitutional provisions, ballot materials, city policies, finance staff, and qualified counsel.

Begin with a revenue map. Group city resources into taxes, fees and charges, intergovernmental revenues, fines and penalties, rents and concessions, investment earnings, and other receipts. Then add financing sources such as bond proceeds or transfers. Financing is not the same as recurring revenue. Borrowing moves resources across time and creates repayment obligations.

Taxes support public purposes and are imposed under governmental authority. California distinguishes general taxes from special taxes. At a high level, general tax proceeds may be used for general governmental purposes, while special tax proceeds are dedicated to specified purposes. Approval rules differ. Never infer authority from the label alone. Ask counsel how the measure is legally structured.

Fees and charges usually connect payment to a service, benefit, use, regulation, or burden. Examples can include utility charges, facility rentals, permits, and service fees. But calling a payment a fee does not settle its legal character. The amount, purpose, payer, methodology, and governing law matter. A cost-of-service study may be essential.

Intergovernmental revenues come from federal, state, county, or other agencies. They may be formula based, competitive, reimbursable, restricted, temporary, or subject to future appropriation. A grant award can be valuable while still creating cash-flow risk, matching requirements, reporting duties, and an eventual funding cliff.

California's fiscal constitution is shorthand for the constitutional and statutory rules that shape local taxing, spending, appropriations, property taxation, assessments, fees, and debt. Several voter-approved propositions have changed that system over time. For a planner, the practical lesson is not to memorize slogans. It is to ask a disciplined set of questions.

Who has authority to impose the revenue?

What approval process applies?

Is the money restricted?

What calculation supports the amount?

Who bears the charge?

How stable is the source?

Can the city adjust it when costs change?

What reporting and accountability follow?

Those questions prevent a common mistake: treating projected revenue as freely available general funding.

Fund accounting reinforces the distinction. A fund is a fiscal and accounting entity used to track resources and obligations. The General Fund typically supports broad governmental services, but cities also use special revenue, capital, debt service, internal service, and enterprise funds, among others. A large balance in one fund may be unavailable for another purpose because of law, contract, accounting structure, grant terms, bond covenants, or council policy.

Use four labels for every significant revenue: restricted, committed, assigned, or otherwise available under the city's reporting framework. Then record whether it is recurring or one-time. These categories answer different questions. A recurring restricted revenue can be stable but unusable for a general service. A one-time flexible receipt can help with a capital purchase but should not casually support permanent positions.

Revenue quality also matters. Evaluate stability, predictability, responsiveness, administration, equity, and public acceptability. A diversified portfolio can reduce exposure to one economic cycle, but diversification does not mean every source is equally desirable. Some revenues vary with retail activity, travel, development, asset values, or service consumption. Some respond slowly to change. Some are costly to administer.

Consider a hypothetical Torrance proposal for a neighborhood resilience center. Staff identifies a one-time state grant, a potential local charge for classes, facility rental income, and General Fund support. Build a revenue table.

For the grant, list award status, eligible costs, reimbursement timing, match, reporting, deadline, and closeout.

For class charges, identify legal authority, cost basis, affordability policy, collection cost, and realistic participation.

For rent, estimate occupancy conservatively and include management, utilities, cleaning, and vacancy.

For General Fund support, show the ongoing amount and what else competes for it.

Then test three years after opening. If the grant ends, rentals underperform, and labor costs increase, does the operating model remain viable? If not, revise scope, identify a durable subsidy, or establish an explicit decision point before the city commits.

Revenue forecasts should distinguish baseline, policy change, and project effect. Baseline is what would likely occur without the proposal. Policy change is the effect of a new rate or rule. Project effect is the incremental result attributed to the project. Do not count the same growth twice.

Forecasts should also use ranges. A single precise number can conceal uncertainty. State key drivers and update them. For a consumption-based charge, drivers may include accounts, usage, rates, exemptions, delinquency, and collection. For a development-related receipt, drivers may include timing, valuation, permits, and legally permitted uses.

When reading a Torrance budget or Annual Comprehensive Financial Report, compare budget basis with reporting basis. Examine notes, fund classifications, transfers, and multi-year trends. The operating budget describes authorized plans. The ACFR reports audited historical financial information. A capital plan organizes investments over several years. Each answers a different question.

Your learner artifact is a Revenue Authority and Reliability Map. Choose one hypothetical city program and list every proposed resource in rows. Use these columns:

Revenue name.

Legal category.

Imposing or awarding authority.

Required approval.

Restricted purpose.

Recurring or one-time.

Economic driver.

Volatility.

Collection or reimbursement timing.

Administrative burden.

Equity concern.

Evidence source.

End with two sentences. First, identify the most dependable ongoing source. Second, identify the source most likely to be misunderstood. Mark unresolved legal questions for counsel instead of answering them yourself.

Now, three recall questions.

Question one: Why can a city not treat every fund balance as available cash?

Answer: Because law, grant terms, contracts, bond covenants, accounting structure, and adopted policy can restrict or commit resources to particular purposes.

Question two: What is the difference between a recurring revenue and an unrestricted revenue?

Answer: Recurring describes duration and repeatability. Unrestricted describes permissible use. A source can be one, both, or neither.

Question three: Why should a forecast separate baseline growth from project effects?

Answer: To measure incremental change accurately and avoid crediting a proposal for revenue that would have occurred anyway.
