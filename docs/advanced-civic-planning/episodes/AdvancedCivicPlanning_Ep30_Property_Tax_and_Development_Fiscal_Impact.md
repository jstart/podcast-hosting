## Transcript

Imagine a former commercial site in Torrance proposed for mixed-use redevelopment. The plan includes homes, shops, public space, and new streetscape improvements. At the hearing, one speaker says the project will transform the tax base. Another says new residents will cost more than they contribute. Both claims may sound confident. Neither is useful without a transparent method.

Development fiscal impact analysis estimates how a project may change city revenues, service costs, capital needs, and risk compared with a no-project baseline.

This lesson is educational preparation. It is not financial, legal, tax, appraisal, accounting, investment, real estate, or municipal advisory advice. California property tax allocation and development finance are fact specific. Use current records and qualified city finance staff, assessors, economists, appraisers, engineers, and counsel for an actual project.

Start with how property tax works at a practical level. California's system is based on assessed value, not simply current market value each year. Change of ownership and new construction can affect assessment. The county collects the basic property tax and distributes revenue among local taxing entities under allocation rules. A city receives only its applicable share, not the entire tax bill.

That point changes the analysis. Never multiply a project's estimated value by a headline tax rate and call the result city revenue. Identify the taxable assessed value, timing, exemptions or exclusions, allocation share, and possible appeals. Confirm the method with authoritative local data.

Separate the existing baseline from the increment. If a site already produces revenue and requires service, the relevant question is the change caused by the proposal. Estimate existing city property tax, sales-related revenue, charges, and service demands. Then estimate the same categories with the project. The difference is incremental fiscal impact.

Use stabilized conditions and the path to stabilization. A development may take years to build and occupy. Construction can generate temporary receipts and temporary service demands. Those should not be mistaken for permanent annual performance. Show entitlement, construction, lease-up, stabilization, and later renewal as different phases.

On the revenue side, consider property tax, sales and use tax where applicable, transient occupancy tax if relevant, utility user taxes if applicable, business-related taxes, franchise revenues, permits, service charges, and other lawful sources. Do not add a category merely because another city receives it. Confirm Torrance authority, allocation, and fund destination.

On the cost side, identify police, fire and emergency medical response, transportation operations, street and signal maintenance, parks, libraries, community services, planning, code enforcement, finance, technology, and general administration. Some costs vary directly with activity. Some increase in steps. One additional resident does not require a fraction of a fire station, but enough cumulative growth may require a new unit, vehicle, facility, or shift.

That is the difference between average and marginal cost. Average cost divides total current cost by a population, household, call, or service unit. It is easy to compute but can misstate the near-term effect of a project. Marginal cost estimates the added cost caused by the change. It requires operational evidence. Ask departments what workload driver matters and where capacity thresholds sit.

Capital impact is separate from operating impact. Development may require intersections, utilities, drainage, parks, facilities, or equipment. Some improvements may be built by the developer. Some may be funded through lawful fees, assessments, district mechanisms, grants, or city resources. Identify who owns the asset, who pays initially, who maintains it, and when replacement occurs.

Avoid fiscal zoning. A positive net revenue estimate is not a complete land-use finding, and a project with public costs may still advance housing, safety, equity, environmental, or legal objectives. Fiscal information should illuminate tradeoffs, not displace the general plan, housing law, environmental review, or public values.

Build the model in five sheets or sections.

First, assumptions. Include units, square footage, value, occupancy, phasing, inflation, service population, and source dates.

Second, baseline. Record what occurs if the proposal is not approved, including existing revenue, existing service cost, likely vacancy, and any required maintenance.

Third, annual operating impact. Estimate incremental recurring revenues and costs by fund.

Fourth, capital and replacement impact. Show improvements, funding responsibility, useful life, and renewal.

Fifth, sensitivity and distribution. Change the assumptions that matter most, and show which funds, neighborhoods, or service users experience the effects.

Here is a hypothetical example, with invented numbers only for method. A redevelopment is assumed to add two hundred homes and twenty thousand square feet of local-serving retail. Do not assign a property tax amount yet. First obtain a defensible assessed-value estimate and the city's applicable allocation. Then estimate stabilized occupancy and taxable sales, avoiding the assumption that every retail dollar is new to Torrance. Some spending may shift from an existing store.

For costs, ask whether current patrol, fire response, street maintenance, and recreation systems have capacity. Suppose staff identifies no immediate facility expansion but expects added annual calls and a signal upgrade in year four. Include both. Then test lower assessed value, slower occupancy, retail substitution, higher labor cost, and an earlier capital threshold.

Report a range, not one magic surplus. Show the annual General Fund effect, effects on restricted or enterprise funds, one-time receipts, city-funded capital, developer-funded capital, and unfunded risk. State which results depend on negotiated terms or future action.

Practical review begins with source discipline. For Torrance facts, use current city budgets, Capital Improvement Plans, public agenda reports, development agreements, fee schedules, and ACFRs. County assessor or auditor-controller information may be necessary for property tax assumptions. Cite each input by document, page or table, fiscal year, and retrieval date.

Ask staff to reconcile the forecast after approval. Compare actual valuation, occupancy, receipts, calls for service, and maintenance with the model. Update the model when phases change. Fiscal impact analysis is more credible when it becomes a learning system.

Your learner artifact is a Development Fiscal Impact Workbook Outline. It need not contain actual confidential data. Create the five sections just described. Add an assumption register with owner, source, range, and update date. Add a double-counting check for baseline growth, shifted sales, pass-through funds, reimbursements, and transfers. Finish with a one-paragraph conclusion that identifies the two assumptions most capable of reversing the result.

Now, three recall questions.

Question one: Why is the full property tax bill not city revenue?

Answer: The county distributes property tax among multiple taxing entities under allocation rules, so the city receives only its applicable share.

Question two: Why can average service cost mislead?

Answer: Many services have available capacity and step costs. The fiscal effect depends on marginal workload and when a capacity threshold is crossed.

Question three: What comparison defines incremental fiscal impact?

Answer: The projected revenues, costs, and capital needs with the project compared with a defensible no-project baseline.
