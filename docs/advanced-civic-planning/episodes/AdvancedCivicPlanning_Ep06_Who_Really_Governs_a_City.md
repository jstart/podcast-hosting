## Transcript

A Torrance resident asks who approved a visible change near a major street. The planning commission discussed it, the City Council funded related work, a regional agency set part of the transportation framework, a private owner controlled the site, and state law limited what could be denied. The honest answer to "Who governs?" is often a network, not one name.

This episode is advanced study preparation, not legal advice. It does not assign authority for any pending Torrance matter. Formal powers, agency relationships, and current officeholders can change. For a live decision, consult the current staff report, City Charter, Municipal Code, adopted budget, agency documents, and the city attorney.

Start with formal institutions. Torrance uses a council-manager form of government under its charter. Voters elect officials as provided by current city law. The council legislates, adopts budgets, sets policy, and performs other charter and statutory functions. The city manager directs administration under the charter and council direction. Departments provide professional and operational capacity. Boards and commissions exercise delegated roles. The city attorney advises and represents the city. The city clerk performs legally significant election, records, and meeting functions.

An organization chart is necessary, but insufficient. Formal authority tells you who may sign, vote, appoint, spend, regulate, or administer. Governance analysis also asks who sets the agenda, controls information, mobilizes constituencies, provides capital, owns land, supplies expertise, implements decisions, and can delay action. Those forms of influence do not all equal legal authority.

MIT OpenCourseWare's Frameworks of Urban Governance encourages analysis of institutions, actors, resources, incentives, and scales rather than treating city hall as a single decision maker. Adapt that framework into five questions. Who has formal authority? Who controls critical resources? Who defines the problem? Who can block or reshape implementation? At what geographic scale does the issue operate?

Consider elected officials. They possess democratic legitimacy and public decision power, but they face legal limits, budget constraints, electoral incentives, incomplete information, and implementation capacity. Professional staff contributes continuity, technical knowledge, and administrative control. Staff does not merely transmit orders. The design of options, timing, data selection, and feasibility analysis can shape what elected officials perceive as possible.

Commissions add specialized public deliberation and can filter, develop, or decide matters under delegated authority. Their influence varies by subject. A recommendation can frame the council's choice even when it is not final. A final permit action can be legally significant even when appealable. A commission's power is strongest when members understand both their jurisdiction and the evidence needed for implementation.

Voters govern directly as well as through representatives. Elections select officials. Initiatives and referenda can make or challenge local law within legal boundaries. Bond and tax measures may require voter approval. Public opinion affects agendas even without a formal vote. Yet the loudest organized constituency may differ from the full electorate, and the electorate may differ from all people affected by city policy.

Private actors govern parts of urban life through ownership and investment. A city may zone land for housing, but owners decide whether and when to propose development. Lenders and insurers shape feasibility. Employers influence travel patterns and fiscal conditions. Business and neighborhood organizations aggregate interests. Nonprofits deliver services and knowledge. Utilities control infrastructure with their own legal duties. Media and digital networks frame public attention.

Influence becomes power when an actor can change another actor's feasible choices. A landowner can withhold a site. A state agency can condition funds. Residents can appeal or litigate. Staff can identify that an option cannot be administered. A council can change zoning. A court can invalidate action. A regional body can allocate funds or establish planning requirements. Power is issue-specific. The actor who dominates a budget question may have little control over an adjudicative permit.

Regional governance is especially important in Torrance. Air, housing markets, watersheds, transit, freight, jobs, and travel ignore city boundaries. Los Angeles County agencies, the Southern California Association of Governments, the South Coast Air Quality Management District, transportation agencies, school districts, special districts, neighboring cities, and state and federal agencies can shape outcomes. Their exact roles differ. Never refer vaguely to "the county" or "the region" when the responsible entity can be named.

Time is another governing resource. A body that controls the agenda can sequence studies, budgets, and votes so that one option becomes feasible before another. Statutory deadlines can transfer leverage from local deliberation to applicants, courts, or state agencies. Map calendars alongside institutions.

Fiscal structure also governs. Revenue sources create incentives and constraints. Sales activity, property values, service costs, pension obligations, restricted funds, grants, and capital schedules affect choices. A city cannot freely move every dollar to every purpose. A grant may define eligible projects. An enterprise fund has a specific financial structure. A one-time payment cannot safely support a permanent program. Ask which fund, which time horizon, and which legal restriction.

Informal norms matter. Who gets called before a proposal is public? Which departments coordinate easily? Does the public expect commissioners to negotiate design from the dais? Are difficult choices postponed until a deadline? Norms can help institutions work, but they can also obscure accountability. Advanced governance analysis makes informal practice visible without assuming it is corrupt.

Apply this to a Torrance corridor strategy. Create an actor map. Put the City Council, planning commission, city manager, relevant departments, city attorney, property owners, tenants, employers, neighborhood groups, transit and regional agencies, utilities, school institutions, and state regulators on the page. For each, list authority, resources, interests, veto points, and implementation duties.

Suppose the goal is safer access and more housing near jobs. Planning may propose zoning. Public works may control street design. Transit service may belong to another agency or require coordination. Utilities may determine upgrade timing. Owners control project initiation. State law sets housing obligations and approval rules. The council controls local legislation and budgets. Residents and workers supply experience and political support or opposition. No single actor can deliver the outcome alone.

Use the map to design strategy, not to assign blame. If a desired bus frequency lies outside direct city control, identify the agency with operational authority, the data it uses, the funding cycle, and the city's available leverage. If zoning capacity exists but projects do not occur, examine land assembly, financing, infrastructure, market conditions, and approval risk. If implementation stalls inside city government, locate the handoff, budget, staffing, or policy ambiguity.

Accountability requires matching claims to power. Do not credit a body for an outcome it could not cause or condemn it for a power it does not hold. At the same time, lack of unilateral control is not lack of responsibility. Officials can convene, negotiate, disclose constraints, advocate to other levels, and design lawful incentives.

Three recall questions follow.

Question one. What five governance questions should you ask?

Answer. Who has formal authority, who controls resources, who defines the problem, who can block or reshape implementation, and at what scale the issue operates.

Question two. Why is an organization chart insufficient?

Answer. It identifies formal roles but not agenda control, ownership, finance, information, mobilization, implementation, and cross-government influence.

Question three. How should accountability be assigned?

Answer. Match claims to actual authority and leverage, while recognizing duties to coordinate, explain, and advocate when control is shared.

Give every actor columns for formal power, practical influence, resources, incentives, dependencies, veto points, and implementation tasks. Draw arrows showing who needs whom.
