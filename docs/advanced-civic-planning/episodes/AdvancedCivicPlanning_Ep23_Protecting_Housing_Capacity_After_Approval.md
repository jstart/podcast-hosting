# Episode 23: Protecting Housing Capacity After Approval

Explains how implementation, replacement capacity, monitoring, and enforcement protect planned housing.

## Transcript

Imagine that Torrance approves a commercial project on a parcel listed in the housing-element sites inventory. The project is popular and economically useful, but it includes no housing. A commissioner says the city already completed its housing planning when the element was certified. Staff opens a capacity ledger and asks the better question: after this approval, do adequate sites remain for the city's unmet housing need at each income level?

This episode is preparation for public service. It is not legal advice about a site, approval, demolition, or capacity calculation. Housing-capacity laws contain technical definitions and deadlines and are frequently amended. Use the current statute, current HCD guidance, the adopted housing element, permit and entitlement records, and advice from the Torrance city attorney.

The sites inventory is a planning claim. It identifies parcels and assumptions showing how the jurisdiction can accommodate its RHNA. Those assumptions include potential units, income categories, realistic capacity, existing use, and expected redevelopment. Time does not freeze after adoption. Parcels develop, applications change density, lot lines move, uses continue, and affordability commitments shift. The inventory therefore needs a living ledger.

The best-known protection is Government Code section 65863, often called no net loss. When a city or county approves development on an inventory site with fewer units by income category than the housing element identified, it must make specified findings about remaining capacity. If the remaining sites are not adequate to accommodate the unmet need, the jurisdiction generally must identify and make available sufficient replacement sites within the statutory period.

Do not reduce this to total units. Income categories matter. Losing capacity assumed for lower-income housing cannot necessarily be cured by unused above-moderate capacity. The zoning and development standards on the remaining or replacement sites must meet applicable requirements for the need being accommodated.

Build a ledger with one row per inventory site. Record the parcel identifier, inventory program, assumed capacity, assumed income category, zoning, realistic-capacity method, current entitlement, building permits, completed units, affordability restrictions, and remaining credited capacity. Add the date of the latest verification and a link to the action that changed the row.

When an approval arrives, ask five questions. Was the parcel in the inventory? How many units and which income categories did the element assign? What does the approval authorize? How much credited capacity remains after the action? Does the city still have adequate sites for the remaining RHNA by income category?

The answer can require legal and planning judgment. A mixed-use approval may preserve some capacity. A nonresidential approval may consume the development opportunity assumed in the element. An entitlement can expire. A phased project can change over time. Never update the ledger from an agenda title alone.

Replacement is not just finding an empty row in a spreadsheet. The site must be made available with appropriate zoning and development standards within the period required by current law. Depending on the context, the city may need to rezone and satisfy by-right or density requirements. The city should not wait until the deadline to discover that environmental review, public notice, or ordinance preparation is required.

Capacity is also protected by the Housing Crisis Act. Current law restricts certain actions that reduce residential development capacity, including downzoning and reductions in intensity, subject to statutory terms and exceptions. It also contains demolition and replacement protections and limits on housing moratoria. The act's provisions and relevant baseline dates have been amended and extended. Treat any summary as a prompt to open the current statute.

Project review creates another risk. For a housing development project protected by the Housing Accountability Act, a city generally cannot deny the project or reduce its density when it complies with applicable objective standards unless demanding findings are supported. Conditions should not quietly remove units in a way that functions as an unlawful density reduction.

Density bonus law can also affect the approved capacity. A qualifying project may receive additional density, concessions, waivers, and parking relief. The local ledger should record the actual approval and affordability, but staff must avoid treating bonus units or statutory relief as evidence that the underlying zoning can be reduced elsewhere without analysis.

Demolition requires its own branch. Count existing protected units, occupied units, units withdrawn from rent or lease, and lower-income tenancy as current law requires. Identify replacement obligations, right-to-return provisions, relocation duties, and timing. A site that appears to add twenty units may have a smaller net effect after existing units are counted. Capacity accounting and tenant protection are related but distinct.

Monitoring should connect planning, building, and finance systems. Planning knows entitlements. Building knows permits and completion. Housing staff know covenants and income restrictions. The city clerk knows final actions. If those records never reconcile, the APR and the capacity ledger can drift apart.

For practical application, use a fictional inventory parcel credited with 100 lower-income units. Scenario A approves 80 deed-restricted lower-income units. Scenario B approves 80 market-rate units. Scenario C approves a warehouse. Do not assume the same result. Complete a worksheet for each scenario identifying the change by income category, remaining jurisdiction-wide need, possible findings, replacement question, and evidence counsel would need.

Commissioners should ask for capacity information before acting on a relevant legislative or discretionary item. The staff report should identify inventory status and the no-net-loss analysis when applicable. If replacement may be needed, ask for the deadline and responsible body. If staff concludes the law does not apply, ask for the short reason and preserve it in the record.

The commission should also receive periodic portfolio reports. A map is useful for orientation, but the ledger controls the arithmetic. Show original inventory capacity, approved capacity, permitted units, lost or changed sites, replacement actions, and remaining buffer by income category. A small buffer deserves attention before it reaches zero.

Do not double count. One parcel should not simultaneously supply full capacity to two incompatible programs. Do not count an expired entitlement as completed housing. Do not count an application as a permit. Do not assume every zoned unit is realistic capacity if the adopted methodology applied a lower factor.

The policy objective is continuity. The city made a planning showing at adoption. Every later decision should preserve an honest path from that showing to available sites, approvals, permits, and homes. A ledger does not force construction. It keeps the city's planning commitments visible.

Details change, and statutory exceptions can be decisive. Current statute and city-attorney advice control every real finding.

Now three recall questions.

Question one. Why is a total-unit count insufficient for no-net-loss monitoring?

Answer. The analysis tracks capacity for the remaining housing need by income category, not only a citywide total.

Question two. What are the five basic questions when an inventory site receives an approval?

Answer. Confirm inventory status, assigned units and income levels, the approved use and units, remaining credited capacity, and whether adequate sites still accommodate the unmet need.

Question three. Does identifying replacement capacity mean merely naming another parcel?

Answer. No. The replacement must satisfy current legal requirements and be made available with appropriate zoning and standards within the applicable period.

Your learner artifact is a capacity ledger template and the three-scenario worksheet. Include fields for source document, action date, income category, remaining capacity, replacement deadline, and legal review. Add one reconciliation line connecting the ledger to the APR.
