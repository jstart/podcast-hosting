## Transcript

A public budget is a prospective policy document. It translates priorities into authority, staffing, contracts, capital work, and service expectations for a defined period.

This episode adapts Kioko and Marlowe's chapter Budget Strategy, published by University of Washington Pressbooks under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 except where otherwise noted. It condenses, paraphrases, and reorganizes the authors' explanatory prose. Third-party cases, examples, figures, tables, quotations, and linked resources are excluded.

A sound process begins with a baseline forecast under current policy. Separate continuing commitments from proposals, recurring resources from one-time resources, and operating needs from capital financing. Document economic, workload, compensation, and implementation assumptions.

The legal budget controls spending, but useful budget information also connects dollars to work. Inputs show resources, outputs show units delivered, and outcomes show conditions the service seeks to improve. Targets should be few enough to manage and clear enough to audit.

Participation and politics are part of budgeting because resource choices distribute benefits, burdens, and risk. Decision packages should show alternatives and service effects rather than invite debate over unexplained line-item changes. Reserves need stated purposes, target ranges, and replenishment rules.

Timing shapes the choice. Information introduced before options narrow can change the baseline, while the same information arriving after commitments form may be treated as a disruption. A good calendar identifies forecast updates, proposal deadlines, public hearings, adoption, implementation, and review. It also names who may revise assumptions and who may authorize amendments.

For a hypothetical budget briefing, start with the current adopted document and latest periodic review from the official budget index. Build a one-page decision table that separates baseline commitments, proposals, one-time resources, recurring resources, service effects, and implementation risks. Use the transparency page only to locate separately labeled dashboards and current tools. Preserve the fiscal year and adoption status on every exhibit. This exercise does not evaluate Torrance's adopted priorities or forecast.

Adoption is not the end. Monitor actual results against a seasonal plan, explain variances, update forecasts, and distinguish timing from structural change. Transparent amendments preserve authority and create a record of why policy moved.
