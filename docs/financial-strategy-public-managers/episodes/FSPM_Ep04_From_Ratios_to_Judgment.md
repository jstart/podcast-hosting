## Transcript

A ratio is a question generator, not a verdict. Financial statement analysis turns accounting information into comparisons across time, plans, and similar organizations. The analyst must still understand definitions, context, and unusual events.

Liquidity measures examine near-term capacity. Current resources relative to current obligations, days of cash, and collection patterns can reveal stress before a year-end deficit appears. Restricted balances should not be counted as freely available, and an annual average can hide a seasonal cash trough.

Operating measures ask whether recurring revenues support recurring services. Trends in operating margin, own-source revenue, and expenditure growth can identify structural imbalance. A one-time grant or asset sale may improve the current result while leaving next year's service plan unsupported.

Solvency concerns longer horizons. Debt burden, pension and other post-employment obligations, capital condition, and revenue concentration all shape resilience. Comparisons require consistent accounting bases and service responsibilities. Two governments with similar ratios may face very different infrastructure, demographic, or legal demands.

Trend selection matters as much as formula selection. A ratio should have a decision purpose, a stable definition, and enough periods to reveal direction without hiding a structural break. Peer comparisons require comparable responsibilities and accounting. A dashboard should link each indicator to a source statement and note, not detach a colored symbol from the underlying record.

Use a small dashboard tied to decisions. Show definitions, denominators, peer-selection rules, and several years of results. Investigate changes rather than assigning automatic red or green labels. Judgment becomes defensible when the reasoning from measure to conclusion is explicit.
