## Transcript

California law defines two kinds of plans. The general plan is the city's long-range constitution for land use, usually looking twenty to thirty years out. A specific plan is a tighter set of standards for one geographic area. Zoning is not a third plan. It is the ordinance that carries the general plan into lot-by-lot rules: uses, height, setbacks, lot coverage, landscaping, signs, and parking. Vision documents and area studies can guide you, but they are not the standard unless the council has adopted them into the plan or the code.

State law requires general plan elements for land use, circulation, housing, conservation, open space, noise, and safety. Where a city has disadvantaged communities, it also needs an environmental justice element. Cities in certain air basins, including the South Coast basin that covers Torrance, also address air quality. The elements have to agree with each other, and the zoning has to agree with the plan. A project that needs the zoning changed is asking for a legislative act, not a routine permit.
Inside the plan, read three layers. A goal is the outcome the city wants. A policy is the commitment that regulates decisions. An action, sometimes called an implementation program, is the to-do list. A hearing that cites a goal and ignores the policy underneath it is arguing with a slogan.

The housing element is the only element the state certifies. The Department of Housing and Community Development reviews it. It runs on an eight-year cycle. It must plan sites, by income category, for the city's share of the regional housing need, and it must name the barriers the city will remove. Every year the city files a progress report. If the housing element and the zoning disagree, the housing element can control until the zoning is brought into line. Torrance's current cycle, 2021 through 2029, requires the city to plan for 4,939 homes, including more than 2,400 for very low and low income households. The state found the element compliant in 2022 on the condition that sites be rezoned, and later warned that late rezoning could cost that compliance.

Traditional zoning, the kind upheld in Euclid, separates uses and regulates intensity and setbacks. Form-based rules care more about the shape of the building, how it meets the street, and the design of the public realm. Torrance still lives mostly in the older system, with important overlays on top. The general plan adopted in April 2010 organizes the city as a deliberate mix of homes, shops, and industry. The Hawthorne Boulevard Corridor Specific Plan, adopted in 1996, covers about eight hundred fifty-seven acres in five districts and still requires a use permit for much of its mixed use. The 2023 Housing Corridor Overlay, about ninety-five acres in seven areas, allows housing by right at specified heights. Downtown, an overlay on the original Olmsted tract sends most new development to this commission, and a 2017 ordinance protects local landmarks.
When a project arrives, name the layer that actually governs it. General plan designation. Zoning. Any specific plan or overlay. Then the state housing laws, which can waive local standards even when every local layer says no. The next episodes are about how to do that reading on a real application.
See the Planning Commission 101 materials from the 2026 academy, and Torrance's adopted general plan, zoning, and specific plans.
