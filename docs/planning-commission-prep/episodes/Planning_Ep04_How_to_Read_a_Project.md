## Transcript

A good commissioner can tell, from the first pages of a staff report, what decision is actually being asked. Use the same pass every time. It comes from the way the Institute for Local Government teaches project review, tightened for a California housing agenda.

First, name the action. Is this ministerial, and therefore not yours? Is it a conditional use permit, a variance, a map, a design review, or a legislative recommendation to change the plan or the zone? The findings you must make are different for each. In Torrance a conditional use permit requires eleven findings, including compatibility with the neighborhood, a suitable site, adequate access and services, and no harmful noise or hazard. Do not borrow those findings for a variance, and do not borrow variance findings for a plan amendment.
Second, test consistency. What is the general plan designation, and what does the zoning allow? If they conflict, say so. For housing, consistency is judged against objective standards: rules an applicant can measure before filing, with no room for a personal judgment call. A height limit of thirty feet is objective. A rule that upper floors must "step back to ease the transition" is not. A court of appeal said so in California Renters Legal Advocacy and Education Fund versus City of San Mateo. If a housing project meets the objective rules, the Housing Accountability Act sharply limits your power to deny it or cut its density.

Third, walk the site without romance. What is next door, and does the use clash with it in a way the code actually regulates: truck noise against bedrooms, a driveway through a sidewalk, grading on a steep slope? Can people reach it on foot, on a bike, in a car, and with a delivery truck, or do the paths cross? Is there a listed historic building, a floodplain, a very high fire zone, or a habitat constraint that a specific written standard addresses? Neighborhood character, standing alone, is not that standard.
Fourth, read the numbers the code uses. Height, setbacks, lot coverage, floor area, parking, private open space, window area on the street. Write down each one the project misses, and then ask whether a density-bonus waiver or concession has already excused it. A missed standard that state law waives is not a denial finding.
Fifth, read the environmental cover sheet. Exempt, negative declaration, mitigated negative declaration, or full impact report. If it is exempt, the fight will be about whether every condition of the exemption is met. If it is not, you will need the baseline, the thresholds, and the mitigation, which is its own episode.

Then look at the economics only as far as the law allows you to. A condition that is legal can still make a project unbuildable. Ask what the condition costs per home and whether a cheaper design meets the same purpose. Torrance keeps a small share of the property tax and a much larger share of sales tax, so replacing a store with apartments is a fiscal question as well as a housing question. Ask staff what the site produces in taxes now and what it would produce after.
Close the pass by drafting, in your notes, the sentence you would say if you voted tonight. If you cannot tie that sentence to a finding and a page of the record, you are not ready, and the right move is a question to staff, not a speech.
