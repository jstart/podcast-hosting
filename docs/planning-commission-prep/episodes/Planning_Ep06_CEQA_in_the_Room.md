## Transcript

The California Environmental Quality Act applies when the city has a discretionary project: a decision that can shape a physical change in the environment. If the approval is ministerial, stop. CEQA is not required. When it does apply, its point is narrow. It tells you how the project changes the environment from conditions on the ground today. It pushes the city to avoid or reduce significant damage. And if you approve the project anyway, it makes you say why, in public.

There are three basic outcomes, and you should be able to name which one is in the packet. An exemption, when the legislature or the guidelines say this kind of project usually needs no study. A negative declaration, or a mitigated negative declaration, after an initial study shows impacts can be avoided or brought below a threshold. Or an environmental impact report, when impacts may be significant and cannot be eliminated by redesign.
The test that forces an impact report is the fair argument standard, and it is deliberately easy to meet. If substantial evidence in the record supports a fair argument that the project may have a significant effect, an impact report is required, even when other evidence points the other way. Substantial evidence means facts, reasonable assumptions based on facts, and expert opinion grounded in facts. It does not mean argument, speculation, or a speech about property values with no physical impact attached. Residents' observations can count. That is why a packed room can change the document, and also why volume alone cannot.

Read three things in whatever document you have. The baseline: the existing conditions the study measures change against. If the baseline is fuzzy, the document is not ready. The thresholds of significance: the line the city uses to decide an impact matters. And the mitigation: a change to the project that responds to a significant impact the document actually found. Every mitigation measure becomes a condition of approval. The reverse is not true. A design condition you add for the code is not CEQA mitigation. And CEQA does not hand the city any power it did not already have.
If a significant impact remains, you do not pretend it is gone. You adopt a statement of overriding considerations that says why the city's other goals justify approving the project anyway. You adopt that environmental action before you adopt the project.

Two 2025 statutes changed the daily work. Assembly Bill 130 created a broad exemption for urban infill housing, generally on sites up to twenty acres that are already urban, consistent with the general plan or the zoning, dense enough to meet half the default density for that kind of city, and clear of the sensitive-site rules. A density bonus does not, by itself, destroy that consistency. The project cannot demolish a historic structure that was already listed when the preliminary application was filed. Builder's remedy sites are tighter: about five acres. Senate Bill 131 says that if a housing project misses an exemption by a single condition, the study looks only at the effect of that condition.
Exempt does not mean silent. For the new infill exemption the city must notify affiliated Native American tribes, give them time to request consultation, finish that consultation, and then approve or disapprove on a short clock. Certain conditions still attach, including a look at hazardous materials on the site, and extra conditions near freeways. Rezonings that carry out an approved housing element have their own exemption.
CEQA will not fix a traffic problem that already exists, and it is not a vote to like the architecture. Ask staff whether the document studied the whole of the project, whether comments were answered, and which threshold each big impact is measured against. Then decide.
The 2026 academy's Introduction to CEQA, and the Association of Bay Area Governments summary of Assembly Bill 130 and Senate Bill 131, are the backbone of this episode.
