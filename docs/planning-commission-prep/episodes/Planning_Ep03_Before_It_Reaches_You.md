## Transcript

Most of a project's life happens before you see it. By the time a staff report is in the packet, planners, public works, the fire marshal, and sometimes outside agencies have already pushed the design through several rounds. Your leverage is highest if you understand that path, and lowest if you try to restart it from the dais.

Start by asking which kind of review this is. If the project meets every objective standard and the code says it shall be approved, it is ministerial. Staff decides it. There is often no hearing, and because there is no discretion, the California Environmental Quality Act does not apply. Accessory dwelling units, many lot splits, and some streamlined apartments never reach a planning commission at all. Administrative approvals, such as a small house or a minor design permit, may stop at staff or a zoning administrator.
Discretionary projects are the ones built for this room: larger apartments and commercial buildings, conditional use permits, variances, subdivisions, and coastal permits where they apply. In Torrance a conditional use permit is a commission decision. A variance is different: the commission holds the hearing and recommends, and the City Council decides.

Housing applications now come with a clock. An applicant can file a preliminary application and lock in the ordinances in effect that day. The city must publish, on the application form itself, the information it needs to call an application complete. Completeness comments on a resubmittal are limited to issues raised the first time. Those rules, born in the Housing Crisis Act, were made permanent in 2025. A housing project that meets objective standards is also limited to five hearings conducted by the city, and continuances and appeals count.
That changes what "getting to yes" can mean. Staff can still ask an applicant to meet the code, fix a fire-access problem, or adjust a driveway. Staff cannot freely trade away units to calm a neighbor if the project already complies. On a density-bonus project the applicant may also be entitled to waivers of development standards. The useful staff question, asked before the hearing, is simple: which standards still bind this project, and which has state law already waived?

The packet is the hearing. Read the recommended findings and the conditions before you study the renderings. Check that any environmental document was circulated and that comments were answered. If you visit the site, say so at the start of the item, and do not talk about the merits with the applicant or the neighbors while you are there. If you are considering a motion the staff report did not draft, call staff before the meeting so the city attorney can tell you whether that motion can be made on this record.
After you vote, if the commission is the final body, conditions, mitigation, and the appeal period take over. In Torrance an appeal to the council must be filed within fifteen days. If you only recommended, the whole noticing and packet cycle starts again upstairs. Impact fees are often paid later, at building permit or certificate of occupancy, and the building is not final until every department signs off that the conditions were actually met.
The academy session Beyond the Hearing is the source for this path. Ask Torrance staff to walk one recent project from first submittal to blue card so you can see which steps this city actually uses.
