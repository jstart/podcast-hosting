## Transcript

The vote is the least important sentence of the night. The findings are the decision. California courts throw out land use approvals and denials that skip the reasoning, even when the outcome was popular.

The case to remember is Topanga Association for a Scenic Community versus County of Los Angeles, decided by the California Supreme Court in 1974. Findings must bridge the analytic gap between the raw evidence and the ultimate decision. In practice that is four habits. Track each required finding in the words the ordinance uses. Point to evidence already in the record: a page of the staff report, a plan sheet, a study, or testimony. Say why the contrary evidence is less persuasive. And if you are denying a project against staff's recommendation, do not invent the findings out loud. Continue the item and ask staff to bring back a written denial resolution you can adopt.
Avoid the adjectives that feel like findings and are not. "Does not fit the neighborhood" and "too intense for this street" are conclusions. They survive only if you can translate them into a standard the applicant could have read in advance, plus evidence that the project misses it. On a housing project that meets objective standards, inconsistency with the general plan or the zoning is not, by itself, the specific health or safety impact the Housing Accountability Act requires before you deny or cut density.

Conditions have their own constitution. Under the United States Supreme Court's decisions in Nollan and Dolan, a condition that takes or demands property must have an essential connection to the project's impacts and be roughly proportional to them. In 2024, in Sheetz versus County of El Dorado, the Court applied that same test to fees set by legislation, not only to fees bargained project by project. A condition also has to be clear enough for a building inspector to enforce six years later. "High quality architecture" is not a condition. "The street-facing wall shall be at least forty percent windows" is.
There is a narrow leftover use for older subjective rules. The 2026 academy noted that subjective standards adopted before January 1, 2020 may still support conditions of approval, if those conditions do not deny the project or reduce its density, and if no later statute has forbidden that use. Treat that as a question for the city attorney on the specific item, not as a general license to redesign the building.

Objective design standards are how a city keeps real design control after the housing laws. State law describes them as standards that involve no personal judgment and that both the applicant and the city can verify against a benchmark known before submittal. The practical test is the verb. "Shall" plus a measurement can be applied. "Should" plus a taste cannot. Useful forms are counts, ratios, and short menus: private open space equal to ten percent of the floor area, or an entry that includes at least two items from a list, each item itself measured. Torrance, outside the housing overlay, still leans on subjective design findings. Writing citywide objective standards is one of the few legislative projects that gives this commission more influence, not less.
When you move, use the staff findings or say exactly which one you are changing and what evidence supports the change. A continuance is justified when that evidence is missing. It is not justified as a way to reopen a policy the council already adopted.
The sources are the academy sessions on Planning Commission 101 and on objective design standards, plus Topanga, Nollan, Dolan, and Sheetz.
