## Transcript

A planning commission does two different jobs, and mixing them up is how good intentions become bad votes. One job writes policy. The other applies policy that is already written. Torrance's commission has seven members, appointed by the City Council, and most nights the second job is the one on the agenda.

The power underneath both jobs is the police power: the city's authority to regulate land for the public health, safety, and welfare. Zoning grew out of that power. Los Angeles adopted an early industrial restriction in 1909. New York adopted the first comprehensive zoning ordinance in 1916. The United States Supreme Court upheld zoning in Euclid versus Ambler in 1926. California required cities and counties to adopt a master plan the next year. Since then California has rewritten planning law faster than almost any other state, mostly because it grew faster.

When the commission recommends a general plan amendment, a zoning change, or a specific plan, it is doing legislative work. It is helping the council decide what the rules should be. That is the moment for vision, for tradeoffs, and for a public argument about what kind of city Torrance ought to be. The commission recommends. The council adopts.
When the commission decides a conditional use permit, a design review, a subdivision, or a similar entitlement, it is doing quasi-judicial work. The rules already exist. The hearing is not the place to renegotiate the density the council adopted, or to substitute one person's taste for the code. The job is to measure the project against the adopted standards, take evidence, and make findings.

Four legal habits protect either kind of decision. Meet in the open, under the Brown Act. Give every party a fair hearing: the applicant, the neighbor, and anyone who wrote a letter. Notice the hearing the way the code requires, because a bad notice is a bad decision. And follow the state laws that now sit on top of the local code. Personal preference is not one of the standards.
A clean motion does three small things. It says what you are approving or denying. It ties that action to the findings the ordinance requires. And it adopts the environmental determination first, then the project. If you need a continuance, take one because the record is incomplete, not because the room is uncomfortable. On a housing project that already meets objective standards, continuances and appeals count toward a five-hearing cap, so a delay is a legal event, not just a scheduling courtesy.
The record you build is the lawsuit, if there is one, and it is also the story the next commissioner inherits. Housing, private investment, and public trust all run through that record. Predictable process is what makes the next honest project show up.
For the legal framework, see the 2026 Cal Cities Planning Commissioners Academy session Planning Commission 101, and confirm Torrance procedure with the city attorney.
